Screen Time Explains 0.4% of Teen Wellbeing. In a Study of 355,358 Adolescents, Wearing Glasses Mattered More

Oxford psychologists ran thousands of defensible analyses across three massive teen datasets instead of cherry-picking one, and the link between digital technology and adolescent wellbeing came out negative, real, and tiny: smaller than the link for wearing glasses, on par with eating potatoes.

By Daniel Voss, Psychology & Neuroscience · September 22, 2026

📋 The Study

Title
The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use
Authors
Amy Orben and Andrew K. Przybylski, 2019 (University of Oxford)
Journal
Nature Human Behaviour, 3(2), 173–182 (peer-reviewed)
DOI
10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1
Sample
n=355,358 adolescents across three large-scale datasets: the UK Millennium Cohort Study, the US Monitoring the Future survey, and the US Youth Risk Behavior Survey (data collected 2006–2016)
Method
Specification curve analysis: thousands of defensible model specifications run instead of a single cherry-picked analysis, drawn from over 600 million possible combinations of measures, covariates, and models
Key Finding
Digital technology use has a negative but tiny association with adolescent wellbeing, explaining at most 0.4% of its variation. Regularly eating potatoes was nearly as negative; wearing glasses was 1.5x more negative; being bullied was 4.3x more negative.
Effect Size
r² ≤ 0.004 (r ≈ 0.06); the authors concluded the effects are too small to warrant policy change
Counterintuition
⚡⚡⚡⚡ 4/5
Replication
Partially replicated and refined: the authors' 2022 longitudinal follow-up found small negative effects concentrated in developmental windows (girls 11–13, boys 14–15). A published 2020 exchange with critics remains unresolved, with honest estimates ranging from tiny to small.

Ask parents at the school gate and the story comes out hardened into consensus. Sometime around 2012, teenagers got smartphones, and teenage mental health fell apart. The graphs line up with cinematic neatness: persistent sadness among American teenage girls climbing through the 2010s, self-harm hospitalizations rising in Britain, loneliness bending upward country after country. Jonathan Haidt turned the pattern into a bestselling book and a policy program. In 2023 the US Surgeon General issued a formal advisory on social media and youth mental health. Schools from New York to London began locking phones in pouches. The mechanism felt obvious. Every parent has watched it: the glowing rectangle, the vanished conversation, the kid who seemed fine until the phone arrived.

Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski, psychologists at the University of Oxford, examined the same underlying data and found the obvious mechanism mostly missing. Their 2019 Nature Human Behaviour paper is the largest and most methodologically paranoid test of the screen-time hypothesis ever run: 355,358 adolescents, three enormous datasets, two American and one British, each analyzed thousands of ways.

The method is the story. Most papers pick one definition of screen time, one definition of wellbeing, one set of controls, and report what emerges. Orben and Przybylski counted the defensible combinations. They found more than 600 million. Then they ran a large sample of all of them and plotted every result. The technique is called specification curve analysis. Its purpose is to make cherry-picking impossible. If an effect appears only under a narrow slice of analytic choices, the curve exposes it. Their curve exposed something else: slice the data however you like, and the link between digital technology and adolescent wellbeing stays negative and very small.

At most, technology use explained 0.4% of the variation in teenagers' wellbeing. The authors translated that abstraction into currencies from the same datasets. Eating potatoes regularly: nearly as negative. Wearing glasses: one and a half times more negative. Smoking marijuana: 2.7 times. Being bullied: 4.3 times. On the positive side, enough sleep and regular breakfast ran 1.7 to 44 times more positive than technology ran negative. Orben's press summary was blunt. Tell me a teenager's screen time, she said, and I still cannot predict their wellbeing with any skill worth naming.

What does 0.4% mean in human terms? The paper never runs this calculation. Its numbers permit it. A variance share of 0.004 means a correlation near 0.06, because 0.06 squared is 0.0036. Assume the two traits form a roughly bell-shaped cloud. Pick two teenagers at random. Bet that the heavier screen user reports lower wellbeing. Your win rate: one-half plus arcsin(0.06) divided by pi. About 0.52. A coin flip wins half the time. Screen time moves you two points past chance. The bell-shaped assumption flatters the screen-time hypothesis, since real survey data is messier. The conclusion survives anyway. The variable the whole debate orbits is, for prediction, nearly inert.

The authors' conclusion matched the math: too small to warrant policy change. It was not their first pass. In a preregistered 2017 study of 120,115 English adolescents, Przybylski and Netta Weinstein found a Goldilocks curve. Wellbeing peaked near one to two hours a day, depending on activity, then declined gently. Moderate engagement, they wrote, is not intrinsically harmful and may be advantageous. The 2019 paper asked whether that result survived every defensible analytic choice. It did.

The Strongest Counterargument

The best case against this paper never disputes its arithmetic. It disputes its relevance. Teen mental health truly cratered around 2012, just as smartphones reached teenage pockets. CDC surveys show persistent sadness and suicidal thinking among American teens, girls especially, climbing steeply through the 2010s. British self-harm admissions for young women rose in parallel. These are not fragile model artifacts. They are large, cross-national, visible in raw trend lines. So if screens explain four-tenths of one percent, either the surveys measure the wrong thing or the damage flows through channels that individual correlations cannot catch: whole peer groups losing sleep and face time together, algorithmic feeds that arrived after most data was collected, network effects where everyone's phone rewires the social world even for light users.

The technical objection has teeth, and it appeared in the same journal. In 2020, Jean Twenge, Jonathan Haidt, Thomas Joiner, and Keith Campbell argued that six analytical decisions in the specification curve, each defensible alone, combined to shrink the estimates. Their exhibit: Kelly and colleagues' analysis of the same British dataset found twice as many heavy social media users as non-users with clinically relevant depressive symptoms, a gap they called large enough to justify concern and possibly policy. For girls, they wrote, one could conclude social media matters more than exercise or even heroin use. Orben and Przybylski replied that variance explained is the honest metric, because mean differences assume away third factors driving both heavy use and low mood. Both sides published. The dispute stands unresolved. That fact is itself informative: honest disagreement spans tiny to small. No defensible analysis makes screens the dominant force the public debate assumes.

The authors' own follow-up concedes part of the point. In 2022, Orben, Przybylski, Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, and Rogier Kievit published a longitudinal study in Nature Communications following 17,409 young people. It found windows of sensitivity: heavier social media use predicted lower life satisfaction a year later for girls aged 11 to 13 and boys 14 to 15, around puberty, and for both sexes at 19. Effects stayed small. Low satisfaction also predicted heavier later use at every age, which undercuts any simple causal story. Still, the timing matters. One average across all adolescence can dilute a real effect concentrated in a vulnerable window. The window the data indicates is early puberty, not the late teens.

What We Didn't Prove

The honest boundaries, stated plainly. This is correlational: it cannot show screens cause anything, either way. Screen time was self-reported, and teenagers misreport like everyone else. Much of the data predates the algorithmic-feed era. As psychiatrist Ben Carter noted at publication, some of it reaches back to 2007, before social media existed in its current form. The study counts hours, not content. An hour video-calling grandparents equals an hour doomscrolling, which is like studying nutrition by weighing food. It cannot capture population-wide displacement, where every device alters the social environment for all. It cannot rule out serious harm to vulnerable subgroups. That is precisely where the 2022 follow-up says to look.

The Bottom Line

Two things hold at once, and the panic demands you pick one. Teen mental health deteriorated badly in the 2010s. The smartphone arrived at the scene. Yet across 355,358 teenagers analyzed thousands of defensible ways, the individual-level link between technology and wellbeing is real, negative, and minute: smaller than wearing glasses, on par with potatoes, dwarfed by bullying, sleep, breakfast. The defensible reading is not that screens are harmless. It is that the hours variable, the thing parents monitor and schools confiscate, was never the main lever. A debate organized around it keeps missing the levers that move.

What You Can Do

Stop optimizing the wrong number. The paper's comparisons are a priority list in disguise. Sleep and breakfast ran up to 44 times more positive than screens ran negative. Bullying ran 4.3 times more negative. A phone rule that protects sleep is evidence-adjacent. A phone rule as moral theater is not. Ask what, not how long. The study counted hours, so its silence on content is total, and clinicians consistently report that content and context outweigh the clock. Time your attention to the windows: early puberty, about 11 to 13 for girls and 14 to 15 for boys, is the peak-sensitivity window the 2022 data identifies. Presence matters most there, not blanket bans for seventeen-year-olds. And if you make policy, take the authors at their word: too small to warrant broad policy change. Spend the capital on bullying, sleep, and the large, boring, unfashionable drivers of adolescent misery. Not the 0.4% one.
