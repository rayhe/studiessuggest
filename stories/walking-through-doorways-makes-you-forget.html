Walking Through a Doorway Makes You Forget What You Came For. A Four-Experiment Replication Found It Only Happens When Your Mind Is Busy

In 2011, Notre Dame researchers found crossing a doorway more than doubled memory errors in the lab. A 2021 replication series of 126 participants found the effect only when people were counting backwards at the same time.

You Know the Feeling

You stand up with a clear purpose, walk into the kitchen, and stop. The reason for the trip is gone. Not faded. Gone, as if the thought belonged to the room you just left, and most of us blame age or distraction, but a group of psychologists at the University of Notre Dame spent years testing a stranger suspect: the doorway itself.

The first experiment was built like a joke: in 2006 Gabriel Radvansky and David Copeland built a virtual building of 66 rooms with the level editor for the video game Half-Life and had volunteers move through it, picking up objects and setting them down, while every so often the screen flashed an object name and asked if the person was carrying it. Information about an object was less available after passing through a doorway than after walking the same distance inside one large room, with distance and time held constant.

Real Rooms, Same Result

A video game corridor is not real life, and Radvansky knew it, so in 2011 he and colleagues Sabine Krawietz and Andrea Tamplin ran the test three more ways, starting with 55 students moving through a 55-room virtual environment, with large rooms twice as long, so distance was identical either way. Asked about an object they should have recognized, people erred 12 percent with no shift and 19 percent after a doorway, while for objects they should have rejected, errors rose from 7 to 22 percent.

Then the team built a real three-room lab, where sixty students put six colored blocks in a box, closed it, and carried it to another table, either across the same room or through a doorway, and after two minutes of arithmetic they answered 12 questions about which blocks were in the box. Errors for objects in the box rose from 23 to 28 percent after a doorway, and from 18 to 21 percent for objects that were not, a smaller effect than in the virtual version.

Run the first experiment’s numbers together and the size of the thing becomes clear, because averaging the two error types, the combined error rate went from 9.5 percent without a shift to 20.5 percent after a doorway, more than double. The pattern was lopsided: errors on objects people should have rejected nearly tripled, from 7 to 22 percent, while errors on objects they should have recognized rose by about half, so a doorway did not so much erase memories as make people claim things that were never there.

Why a Doorway Closes a Chapter

The obvious explanation is context, since you formed the thought in one room and now you are standing in another, so the cues that would have retrieved it are behind you, yet Radvansky’s third experiment was designed to kill that explanation, and it did. Some participants walked through two doorways and ended up back where they had started, and if context were the whole story, returning home should have restored memory. It did not.

The account the authors favor is the event horizon model: the mind does not record experience as one continuous stream but chops the flow into episodes, and a doorway signals the end of one episode and the start of another, so the current model gets filed away and a fresh one opens. The intention you formed in the old room stays filed with the episode it belonged to, and event segmentation is well supported, shaping how people remember films and everyday sequences.

The Twist: Boundaries Can Help Memory Too

Here the story takes a turn, because in 2016 Kyle Pettijohn and Radvansky asked whether the same boundary that hurts retrieval could help encoding, and in one experiment 37 students memorized lists of 12 words on a computer, closing the window halfway through on some lists and opening a new one for the second half. Those lists were recalled a little better, about 37 percent of words against 34 percent, and with stories the gap grew, so two event shifts beat one and one beat none, though a 2026 follow-up found the benefit faded quickly, so even the helpful version is fragile.

The Strongest Counterargument

The case against the popular version is strong, and it comes from a direct attempt to repeat it: in 2021 Jessica McFadyen and colleagues at the University of Queensland and Bond University ran four experiments across virtual reality with and without a counting task, passive video, and real walking through a curtained hallway, and across 126 analyzed participants they found no significant effect of doorways on forgetting. The one exception was the VR experiment with the counting task, where doorways increased false alarms without changing hit rates, and their conclusion sits in the title: doorways do not always cause forgetting.

That failure does not prove the original finding false, since small changes in the task could matter and the older studies were small, but the version of the story that circulates online, that doors wipe your memory, claims far more than the evidence supports. The effect, if it exists at all, is small, shows up most clearly in virtual environments and under cognitive load, and looks less like erasing a thought than like making the mind accept a wrong one. The honest summary: a doorway can act as an event boundary, and under the wrong conditions that boundary costs you a thought. The doorway alone is not enough.

What We Didn’t Prove

No study in this line ever tested the exact thing you experience in your kitchen: participants were probed on objects they had just handled, not on intentions like why they walked into a room, and the samples were almost entirely undergraduates. The real-room experiment found a smaller effect than the virtual ones, and the 2021 replication found none at all without an added memory load, so nothing here shows that doorways explain everyday forgetting in general, or tests long-term memory. What the evidence does establish is that event boundaries change what is retrievable in the seconds after they occur: real in the lab, fragile outside it.

The Bottom Line

Walking through a doorway can make you forget, but the door is only half the story, since the original experiments found memory errors more than doubling after a crossing, and returning to the room where the thought began did not bring it back. A four-experiment replication found the effect only when people were counting backwards, which suggests the doorway matters most when your mind is already busy. The boundary closes a chapter, and whether you lose the thought depends on how much else you were carrying when you crossed it.

What You Can Do

When a thought matters, say it out loud before you cross the threshold, because speaking an intention moves it from the event model you are about to file into a form that survives the boundary. If you are already standing in the kitchen with nothing in your head, retrace your steps. Use boundaries on purpose while you work: close the browser window between topics when you study, and when you are carrying an important errand in your head, do not also carry a phone call, a podcast, and a mental grocery list. The doorway costs you most when your working memory is already full, so cross it with your hands, and your head, as empty as you can manage.
