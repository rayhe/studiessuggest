Participants Said They'd Pay to Avoid an Electric Shock. Then 67% of Men Chose It Over Fifteen Minutes Alone with Their Thoughts.

In 11 studies at the University of Virginia and Harvard, people found sitting quietly with nothing to do but think so unpleasant that many preferred to give themselves a painful electric shock they had previously said they would pay money to avoid.

The Luxury Nobody Wants

We crave solitude the way we crave dessert: in theory, constantly. Every wellness influencer, meditation app, and airport bookshop promotes quiet reflection as essential self-care, the kind of deep restoration that no screen can provide and no schedule should crowd out. Take fifteen minutes. Sit with your thoughts. The mind, left to wander freely, will compose, remember, fantasize, and restore.

Timothy Wilson, a social psychologist at the University of Virginia, believed this too. He and Daniel Gilbert at Harvard set out to study what happens when you give people what they say they want: time alone to think, with nothing to interrupt them.

It went badly.

Eleven Experiments, One Consistent Result

Wilson's team asked participants to sit alone in an unadorned room for six to fifteen minutes with no phone, no book, no music, and no company besides their own thoughts.

In the first six studies, participants rated their enjoyment of the thinking period at 4.55 on a nine-point scale, below the midpoint, and nearly 58% found it difficult to concentrate on their own thoughts even when nothing competed for their attention. When given the choice between thinking and mundane external activities like reading a news article or watching a short video, subjects rated those activities roughly twice as enjoyable as their own unstructured thoughts (Wilson et al., 2014). These were not people deprived of sleep, stressed by deadlines, or placed in uncomfortable chairs; they were given exactly the kind of reflective pause that half a billion dollars' worth of mindfulness apps promise to deliver.

The researchers then raised the stakes in a way that produced the study's most striking result. In study ten, participants sat alone in a lab room for fifteen minutes with a single additional option: a button that would deliver a mild but painful electric shock. Before the experiment began, every participant experienced the shock and was asked if they would pay money to avoid receiving it again, and all of them said yes.

Left alone with their thoughts, 67% of men and 25% of women pressed the button anyway. One participant shocked himself 190 times.

Not a Generational Affliction

The obvious objection is that this is a smartphone-era problem, that constant connectivity has eroded our capacity for quiet contemplation. Wilson anticipated it by recruiting a community sample aged 18 to 77 in study nine, deliberately avoiding the college-student convenience pool that plagues psychology research. Older adults showed no greater fondness for sitting alone with their thoughts than undergraduates did, a finding Wilson interpreted plainly: "The mind is designed to engage with the world, and without training in meditation or thought-control techniques, most people would prefer to engage in external activities."

To rule out lab anxiety as a confound, participants in study eight performed the thinking period at home, where they could sit in their own chair, in their own room, at their own pace. A third of them admitted to cheating by checking their phone, turning on music, or getting up to do something else, and those who complied with the instructions reported no more enjoyment than those sitting in the laboratory (Wilson et al., 2014).

The World Replicated It

Five years later, Nicholas Buttrick and colleagues ran the definitive cross-cultural test, replicating Wilson's study eight across 2,557 participants at twelve sites in eleven countries spanning five continents, including the United States, Belgium, Brazil, Costa Rica, Japan, Korea, Portugal, Serbia, and Turkey (Buttrick et al., 2019). In every single country, participants randomly assigned to do a mundane activity reported significantly greater enjoyment than those assigned to think for pleasure, with the effect large and consistent across all cultures tested.

The researchers identified five individual differences that predicted how much discomfort a person felt: need for cognition, openness to experience, meditation experience, initial positive mood, and phone usage. But these factors explained variation within the effect rather than eliminating it, because no population tested anywhere on the planet enjoyed unstructured thinking as much as doing something.

An Original Calculation

If thinking for pleasure is reliably unpleasant across cultures and age groups, the economic alignment with the attention economy is worth quantifying: Sensor Tower data indicates that the average American unlocks their phone 96 times per day with median sessions under 30 seconds, and if even a quarter of those unlocks are prompted by the discomfort of momentary unstructured thought rather than purposeful intent, that yields approximately 7.4 billion daily app engagements across 310 million American smartphone users driven not by desire for content but by aversion to its absence, which means the attention economy is not competing with other activities for your time so much as harvesting at industrial scale the very discomfort Wilson documented in a bare University of Virginia lab room.

Why the Mind Fights Itself

Wilson proposed that the human mind evolved to monitor and respond to the external environment, and that unguided internal thought is simply not what the system was built for. This aligns with default mode network research: the brain at rest is not serene. It churns through self-referential processing, future simulation, and social cognition, metabolically expensive operations that often leave people less happy than focused attention does. Killingsworth and Gilbert found in a 2010 Science paper that people's minds wander 46.9% of waking hours, and that mind-wandering consistently made them less happy than being focused, regardless of the activity (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010).

Meditation practitioners do learn to tolerate and even enjoy undirected thought, but this requires extensive training, and Buttrick's replication confirmed that meditation experience was positively correlated with thinking enjoyment. The discomfort Wilson documented appears to be real but trainable, which means the instinctive avoidance most people practice is a failure of skill rather than a fixed feature of human cognition.

The Strongest Case Against

The experimental setup is artificial, and nobody in ordinary life sits in an empty room with a shock button wired to their wrist. Critics, including a Frontiers commentary, argued that the demand characteristics of the lab pushed participants toward the shock: they were told they could press it, which may have framed it as an implicit instruction or a curiosity challenge rather than a genuine preference for pain over thought. The 190-shock outlier supports this reading, because if the goal were escape from boredom, one shock would suffice.

The gender gap also complicates the narrative, with men shocking themselves at nearly triple the rate of women. Wilson attributed the difference to sensation-seeking traits that run higher in men, but it could equally reflect gendered compliance with experimental authority or different thresholds for admitting boredom. And the thinking period was forced and structureless, with participants told to think but given no prompt, no topic, and no goal; directed reflection such as gratitude journaling or guided reminiscence might yield different results entirely.

What We Didn't Prove

The shock-specific finding rests on study ten, which included only 42 undergraduates. The broader discomfort with unstructured thinking is robustly established across eleven studies and the Buttrick cross-cultural replication, but the precise shock-preference percentages should be treated as indicative rather than population-level estimates.

The study does not establish that unstructured thought is inherently harmful, only that it is reliably unpleasant in controlled conditions where the alternative is doing nothing at all. Whether chronic avoidance of unstructured thought carries long-term cognitive or psychological costs remains an open question, though the meditation literature suggests the skill of sitting with one's own mind has measurable benefits for those who develop it.

The Bottom Line

The discomfort transcends age, culture, and setting. It is strong enough that a majority of men will administer a painful electric shock they previously said they would pay to avoid rather than sit quietly for a quarter of an hour.

Every meditation teacher who says "just sit" is asking you to do something your brain categorically does not want to do, which is not a reason to skip the practice but may be the strongest argument for why it matters.

What You Can Do

Recognize phone-reaching as escape behavior. The next time you reach for your phone during a quiet moment, notice whether you want to do something specific or simply cannot tolerate the silence. That distinction is the beginning of the skill Wilson's participants lacked.

Start with two minutes, not fifteen. The studies asked people to jump from constant stimulation to a quarter-hour of nothing, and even the at-home condition produced cheating in a third of participants. Meditation research shows that even brief daily practice builds the tolerance for undirected thought. Two minutes of sitting without input is harder than it sounds and far easier than fifteen.

Give your thinking structure. The study's participants were asked to "entertain themselves with their thoughts" but given no direction whatsoever. Research on structured reflection, including gratitude exercises and expressive writing, consistently shows mental health benefits. The problem Wilson documented is not that thinking is inherently aversive; it is that thinking without a handrail can feel like falling.
