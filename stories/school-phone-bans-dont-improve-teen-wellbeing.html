School Phone Bans Don't Improve Teen Wellbeing. 1,227 Students Across 30 Schools Found the Bans Changed Nothing Except Where Kids Use Their Phones

In 30 English schools, 1,227 students showed no difference in mental wellbeing, anxiety, depression, or grades between strict phone bans and permissive policies. Bans cut in-school use by 40 minutes a day, but total screen time did not budge.

The Policy Everyone Agrees On

Few education policies enjoy the consensus of the school phone ban, which lawmakers from both parties describe as a driver of the youth mental-health crisis: roughly half of U.S. states plus Washington, D.C. have now adopted bell-to-bell bans that lock phones away from the first bell to the last. Parents largely back them. Teachers describe the end of confiscation battles and classrooms that finally stay calm, which explains why parent surveys show majority support for the strictest versions. The logic feels airtight: phones in school mean distraction, social media drama, and less attention; remove the phones, and wellbeing and grades should improve. When a policy commands this much bipartisan consensus, the evidence check is usually a formality confirming what everyone already believes, yet the largest study of its kind returned a verdict no legislature expected.

30 Schools, 1,227 Teens, Zero Difference

Victoria Goodyear and colleagues at the University of Birmingham ran the SMART Schools study, comparing 1,227 students ages 12 to 15 across 30 English secondary schools. Twenty schools ran restrictive policies: phones banned for recreational use during the school day, turned off and stowed in bags or pouches, handed in at reception, or barred from campus entirely. Ten ran permissive policies where phones could be used at breaks and lunch or in designated zones. The primary outcome was the Warwick–Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale, a validated instrument, alongside anxiety, depression, sleep, physical activity, classroom behavior, and English and Maths attainment.

The bans worked at exactly what they literally did, since students in restrictive schools spent about 40 fewer minutes per day on their phones during school hours (adjusted mean difference −0.67 hours, p=0.00024) and about 32 fewer minutes on social media (−0.54 hours, p=0.00018), which is not nothing, but on every outcome that motivated the bans, the two groups were statistically indistinguishable. Mean wellbeing landed at 47 in both groups, and the adjusted difference of −0.48 points (95% CI −2.05 to 1.06, p=0.62) was a statistical shrug. Anxiety, depression, sleep, physical activity, classroom disruptiveness, and attainment showed no significant differences either. The confidence interval matters here: the most optimistic end of the range, +1.06 points on a scale with standard deviation 9, rules out a true benefit larger than about 0.12 standard deviations. The study did not just fail to find an effect; it effectively ruled out a meaningful one.

The Phones Just Moved

Why did removing phones from school change nothing about teenagers? Because the phones were not removed from teenagers. Across both groups, average smartphone use ran four to six hours a day. Students in restrictive schools used their phones less during school hours and made up every minute of it elsewhere: total weekday and weekend use was identical between the groups. Forty minutes a day, five days a week is roughly 3.3 hours weekly, and that entire block simply migrated to evenings and weekends. That migration pattern is the finding that should rewire policy. The intervention failed exactly where the underlying theory holds: the same study confirmed heavier smartphone and social media use was associated with worse wellbeing, more anxiety and depression, less sleep, more disruptive behavior, and poorer grades, so the diagnosis was never the problem. The treatment just does not reach the patient, because phone time is fungible across the day. Restrict the 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. window and the 3 p.m. to midnight window absorbs it.

The Strongest Counterargument

Stated at full strength: SMART Schools is cross-sectional, not a randomized trial, so it cannot prove bans do nothing. Schools that ban phones may differ from the rare schools that do not in ways no regression can fully capture, and in the study's sampling area restrictive policies were the overwhelming norm (roughly 93% of schools), making the permissive schools the unusual minority. Confounding could mask a real benefit, and there are genuine upsides the study never set out to maximize. A 2024 meta-analysis of five international studies (Böttger & Zierer) found smartphone bans produce a small but significant improvement in social wellbeing (d=0.162), driven mainly by reduced bullying and conflict during breaks, while a 2026 cost-utility analysis of the same SMART Schools cohort found restrictive policies save schools about £94 per pupil per academic year, almost entirely from less staff time spent policing phone behavior. Less bullying and fewer teacher-hours confiscating devices are genuine wins, even when the mental-health data refuse to move.

The honest synthesis is that bans plausibly improve the social climate of the school day, while leaving total screen time and the mental-health ledger completely untouched. A policymaker promising better teen mental health from a bell-to-bell ban is claiming an effect the best available evidence does not support. A principal wanting calmer hallways has a better case.

What We Didn't Prove

The design was observational: no school was randomly assigned its policy, and there were no before-and-after measurements around a new ban, so causal claims stay tentative. Phone and social media time were self-reported, and teenagers are unreliable witnesses to their own screen time. The comparison rested on 30 schools with a 20-to-10 imbalance, and all of them were in England, so generalization to American bell-to-bell regimes and to very different cultures is extrapolation, not fact. The cost analysis leaned heavily on reports from one senior staff member per school. Nobody measured what teenagers did with the displaced 40 minutes in the evening, the window where the study found the associations between heavy use and worse mental health.

The Bottom Line

School phone bans do exactly what they say and fail at what they are for: they remove phones from the school day without removing them from teenagers' lives, which is why teen mental health, sleep, and grades do not move at all. The most policy-relevant null result in recent education research says the lever everyone pulled attaches to nothing. If the goal is genuinely less phone time rather than less visible phone time, the interventions have to follow teenagers home.

What You Can Do

If you are a parent, treat the evening as the main arena, since it is where the missing 3.3 weekly hours go and where the study implicates total use most strongly: charge phones outside bedrooms overnight, keep devices out of reach during homework hours, and model the same restraint yourself, because household-wide rules outlast school-day ones for the simple reason that they cover the whole week.

If you run a school, be honest about what a ban buys. Fewer in-class disruptions, less break-time bullying, and less staff time spent on enforcement are defensible goals, and the £94-per-pupil staff-time estimate gives you a budget argument. But do not sell a ban to parents as a mental-health intervention, because the evidence does not back that claim.

If you write policy, fund whole-day interventions instead of whole-day bans, because a ban confined to school hours is a visible action that leaves the underlying numbers untouched, and because digital literacy curricula, family media plans, and evening programs have to accompany any restriction given that the 3.3 displaced weekly hours land somewhere, and right now they land in the bedroom at midnight.
