Placebos Keep Getting Stronger in U.S. Drug Trials. The Sugar Pill Now Relieves 30% of Pain

A 2015 analysis of 84 trials found the placebo response in American neuropathic pain trials climbed to a 30% pain reduction by 2013 while drug responses stayed flat. The same pattern has since turned up in antipsychotic, antidepressant, and blood pressure trials, and nobody agrees on why.

The Control Group That Started Winning

Every drug trial has a control group that takes a sugar pill, and the pill does nothing. The drug has to beat it; the size of that win is the whole point. For decades, researchers treated the sugar pill as a fixed yardstick, and it was not supposed to move.

It moved. In 2015, McGill researchers pulled together 84 published trials of drugs for neuropathic pain, the burning nerve pain of diabetes, shingles, and spinal injury, covering 1990 to 2013. In American trials, patients on placebo reported steadily more pain relief as the years passed, and by 2013 the average patient taking a fake pill reported 30 percent less pain, while the patients taking real drugs reported the same relief they always had, and the gap shrank.

The line for American trials starts near zero in the late 1990s and slides to roughly 30 percent pain reduction by 2013. The line for trials run everywhere else stays flat. Something about American trials changed, and the placebo arm changed with it.

The trials changed shape at the same time. In the United States, the average trial stretched from four weeks in 1990 to twelve weeks in 2013, and the average trial grew from fewer than 50 patients to more than 700. Longer and larger trials both came with larger placebo responses. The two arms also improve on different schedules: a real drug reaches its full effect within about four weeks and then plateaus, while a placebo keeps gaining ground for weeks after that. Run a trial for twelve weeks, and you hand the sugar pill eight extra weeks to catch up.

It Was Not Just Painkillers

The pattern was not confined to pain: a year earlier, Columbia researchers did the same exercise for antipsychotic drugs, pooling 105 trials from 1960 to 2013. In the 1960s, the average patient on placebo got worse by 3.5 points on the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale; by the 2000s, the average patient on placebo improved by 3.2 points, while improvement from an effective dose fell from 13.8 points to 9.7.

In the 1960s, the drug beat placebo by 17.3 points; by the 2000s, the margin was 6.5 points, a drop of about 62 percent. Patients needed to detect a difference grow as the square of the shrinking gap, so a trial chasing the modern margin needs roughly seven times as many participants as one chasing the 1960s margin: take 17.3, divide by 6.5, square the result, and you get 7.1. That is why modern trials enroll hundreds where the old ones enrolled dozens, and still come up empty.

Depression showed the same drift: a 2002 analysis of 75 trials found the placebo response in major depression growing from 1981 to 2000, and a 2012 review of three decades of antidepressant trials found drug-placebo differences shrinking as trials got bigger. Blood pressure joined the list in 2018, when 63 trials and 32,022 patients showed the placebo response rising from 1990 to 2016. There, the drug response rose too, so the gap held. Four conditions, four literatures, one direction.

In 2001, Farrar and colleagues pooled 2,724 patients from ten pain trials to ask what improvement patients themselves called meaningful. The answer was a drop of about two points on a ten-point scale, or about 30 percent. That became the field's standard for a clinically important difference. The placebo arm in American pain trials now reaches that bar on its own. The sugar pill alone now delivers what the field defines as a meaningful improvement.

The Strongest Counterargument

The sharpest reply is that nothing about human biology changed; the placebo did not get stronger at all. The trials got leakier. Steven Novella, writing in Science-Based Medicine, called the growing placebo response largely an artifact of how trials are designed and run. David Colquhoun went further, arguing that most of what happens in a placebo arm is not a placebo effect at all but regression to the mean, people enrolling when pain is at its worst and drifting back toward average, plus the natural course of illness and the attention of being in a trial. Strip those out, and the true effect of expectation is small.

That reading has teeth, and its strongest version asks a question Novella posed directly: if newer trials show smaller drug-placebo differences because trials got better, then the old studies were too generous to drugs and the shrinking gap is progress, but if trials got worse, then good drugs are buried by sloppy methods, and the data cannot tell those two stories apart. Neil O'Connell, a pain researcher, offered a third reading: the real story is not that placebos grew, but that the drugs stagnated, and their benefit is smaller than we thought and not getting better.

The artifact story also has specific suspects: longer trials give the placebo arm more time to catch the drug arm, which plateaus at week four. Bigger trials run at more sites, with less experienced staff and looser criteria. Recruitment firms paid per head have an incentive to enroll people who exaggerate baseline pain, which then falls back toward normal on its own. Each pushes the placebo line up without any change in the pill itself. And Colquhoun's blunt coda still stands: if a treatment cannot beat a dummy pill, the trial did not fail; it answered the question.

What We Didn’t Prove

This is a meta-regression across published trials, not an experiment, so it shows the placebo response rose alongside longer and bigger trials, not that length and size caused it. The headline trend is American and covers neuropathic pain, one corner of medicine. Placebo response is a bucket term for everything in the control arm, not the placebo effect, the part driven by expectation. The 30 percent is a trial average, not a promise for any patient. The data end in 2013, though a 2026 review noted the trend continued through 2017. Nobody has shown what happened after that.

The Bottom Line

The yardstick moved, and whether the sugar pill grew stronger or the trials grew leakier, the gap between drug and placebo has narrowed until a placebo arm can clear the bar for a meaningful improvement on its own, and a trial built for the 1960s margins needs seven times the patients to find the margins of today. That is not an argument against painkillers, but one that the way we test them needs the same scrutiny as the drugs themselves.

What You Can Do

Ask for the margin, not the headline: when a new pain drug is announced as effective, look for how much better it did than placebo in absolute terms against the 30 percent bar. Keep taking prescribed medication: a rising placebo response says nothing about whether your drug works for you. If you join a trial, know what the control arm can do: in a modern American pain trial the placebo group alone can reach the threshold for meaningful improvement, so design matters as much as the molecule, and if you design trials, mind the clock, since Tuttle's time courses show the drug's advantage peaks within four weeks, so every extra week mostly helps the placebo.
