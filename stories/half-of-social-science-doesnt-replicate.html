Half of Social Science Didn't Survive a Seven-Year Retest. 274 Claims Redone, and the Survivors Came Back Half as Strong

The DARPA-funded SCORE project had 865 researchers redo 274 published claims from 54 leading journals, with a median 99.6% power to detect the original effects. Just 55% held up, and the typical survivor explained one-sixth as much variance as advertised.

By Theo Aldridge, Social Science · October 9, 2026

Title
Investigating the replicability of the social and behavioural sciences
Authors
Tyner AH, Abatayo AL, Daley M, et al. (2026), led by Andrew H. Tyner with 291 co-authors

Consistent in direction with the 2015 Open Science Collaboration (36% of 100 psychology studies) and Camerer et al. 2018 (62% of 21 papers in Nature/Science)

A finding in a top journal carries an implicit promise. Peer review is the hardest filter in science, so a study that clears it earns a special status: readers cite it, lecturers teach it, policymakers build on it. The working assumption, rarely stated and almost never tested at scale, is that most of these findings would hold up if someone ran them again. Three papers in Nature just tested that assumption at a scale nobody had attempted, and the assumption did not survive the encounter, because when 865 researchers spent seven years redoing 274 published claims drawn from 54 leading journals, roughly half of those claims failed outright and the survivors came back dramatically smaller than advertised.

The project is SCORE, Systematizing Confidence in Open Research and Evidence, funded by the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency and coordinated by the Center for Open Science. Over seven years, 865 collaborators began with 3,900 papers across 62 journals and selected 274 specific claims from 164 quantitative papers published between 2009 and 2018 in 54 journals, spanning twelve fields from economics and criminology to psychology and sociology.

The retest was built to be fair to the originals. Each replication carried a median 99.6% power to detect the original effect size, so a miss cannot be dismissed as an underpowered shrug, and teams used the original materials when available, preregistered their protocols, and had those protocols peer-reviewed in advance through a standardized internal process. Andrew H. Tyner led the replication effort with 291 co-authors. If the reported effects were real at the reported sizes, this machinery would find them.

It found about half, with replications returning statistically significant results in the original pattern for 151 of 274 claims: 55.1%, with a 95% confidence interval of 49.2 to 60.9, and 49.3% at the paper level when weighted for papers contributing multiple claims. Rates varied modestly across disciplines, from 42.5% to 63.1%, with high uncertainty on some field estimates. The plain reading is the uncomfortable one: the odds that a published claim in these journals survives a serious retest are roughly a coin flip.

The misses are only half the story, because the hits shrank. Across claims measured with Pearson’s r, the median effect size was 0.25 in the originals and 0.10 in the replications, an 82.4% reduction in shared variance. Here is the arithmetic the paper invites but never runs in plain language: an r of 0.25 explains about 6% of behavioral variance, while an r of 0.10 explains about 1%, which means the typical published effect accounted for roughly six cents on the dollar of the behavior it described and, after replication, about one cent. The literature was not only half wrong. Its surviving half was about one-sixth as strong as advertised.

The headline number is softer than it looks, and the authors say so themselves. They applied thirteen different methods for judging replication success, and the answers ranged from 28.6% to 74.8%, with 49.3% as the median. They also sampled only positive results, so some shrinkage toward zero is expected from regression to the mean, a caveat they state explicitly. The literature’s defenders have real material here, but every one of the thirteen methods found substantial non-replication, so the direction is not negotiable.

The replication paper did not travel alone. Miske and colleagues tested reproducibility in a second Nature paper: given the original data and code, can you recompute the result? From a stratified sample of 600 papers, only 144 authors, 24%, provided usable data at all, and of the 143 studies that could be evaluated, 53.6% reproduced exactly and 73.5% approximately. Sharing decided it: when data and code were shared in advance, reproduction reached 88%; when reconstructed from partial information, it collapsed to 11%. Aczel and colleagues tested analytical robustness by handing the same data to different analysts: for each of 100 studies, at least five independent reanalysts worked the same claim, with only 34% landing within a narrow tolerance of the original effect, 57% within a fourfold-wider one, and 74% agreeing on the conclusion. Three separate gauntlets, and three middling survival rates.

Do the reader arithmetic: of the 274 claims retested, 123 failed outright, so reading a hundred social-science findings a year from these journals means roughly forty-five will be the kind that would not survive a retest, while the survivors explain a fraction of what they claim. The funder’s motive makes it concrete, since SCORE began because defense planners wanted to know which social science to trust, and the answer is to assume half and verify the rest. The team also tried to train AI models to predict which papers would replicate, hoping for a cheap credibility filter, but the models were not reliable enough to use, so no shortcut exists.

The strongest defense starts with the number itself: thirteen methods, thirteen answers, 28.6% to 74.8%, where the binary criterion behind the headline is a harsh judge. A replication returning p = 0.06 on a real but smaller effect counts as a failure, though the effect may exist, and the authors acknowledge the regression problem directly. Second, there is no exact replication in the social sciences: different participants and contexts produce different outcomes, a point Nature’s own commentary stressed, and no field was completely broken. Third, the sample is a decade old, because papers from 2009 to 2018 predate the open-science reforms, preregistration badges, reporting guidelines, and data-sharing mandates that the 2015 crisis triggered, so this audit photographs the literature before the response, a response that may have improved the picture and has not yet been audited. Fourth, some misses are context rather than error: a finding that depends on a cultural moment or a hidden moderator is a boundary condition the original authors never knew they had, not fraud, and the critics who made this case against the 2015 psychology project were never fully answered.

What We Didn’t Prove

  • The 55% figure is the headline from thirteen defensible methods, not a physical constant. Under the most forgiving criteria, roughly three-quarters of claims counted as replicated.
  • A failed replication is not proof the original effect is zero. Many replications were powered for the reported effect size, not the smaller true effect, so some misses may be real-but-weaker effects the retest could not resolve.
  • The sample covers 2009–2018. It measures a decade-old literature and says nothing direct about whether post-2015 open-science reforms have improved matters.
  • Replications used original materials “when relevant and available.” Where materials were unavailable or methods had to be adapted, fidelity is a judgment call, and reasonable people can dispute individual verdicts.
  • Only published positive claims were sampled. The literature’s unpublished nulls are invisible to this design, so the audit describes the visible literature, not everything ever run.
  • The program’s funding came from DARPA, initially to inform the U.S. military’s use of social-science research. There is no evidence this shaped the findings, but the origin is part of the record.

The Bottom Line

Seven years and 865 researchers redid 274 published social-science claims at near-perfect statistical power, and roughly half held up, while the survivors came back with about one-sixth of their advertised explanatory power. That does not make the field noise, since no discipline was completely broken and every honest counting method lands in the same uncomfortable middle. But the implicit promise of a top-journal byline, that someone checked, is worth about fifty cents on the dollar. Until the reform-era literature gets its own audit, read every finding as a candidate rather than a conclusion.

What You Can Do

  • Check for data and code before you trust a paper. In the SCORE reproducibility arm, papers that shared both in advance reproduced 88% of the time, versus 11% when data had to be reconstructed. An OSF or GitHub link is the single strongest credibility signal the project found.
  • Halve the reported effect size in your head. The median replication shrank from r = 0.25 to r = 0.10. When planning a policy, product, or classroom intervention on a published finding, budget for the smaller number.
  • Prefer preregistered replications and meta-analyses over single splashy studies. One unreplicated positive result is a rumor with a p-value; a preregistered replication is evidence.
  • If you fund or commission research, demand replication for high-stakes claims. SCORE’s funder wanted to know what to trust, and the audit’s existence is the model: verification is a line item, not a luxury.
  • Teach the thirteen-number spread, not the headline. Any single replication rate is a methodological choice. The honest statement is that every counting method found substantial non-replication, and the range reflects genuine ambiguity about what counts.
