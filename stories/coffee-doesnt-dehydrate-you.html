Your Morning Coffee Doesn't Dehydrate You. A 2014 Counterbalanced Trial of 50 Men Found 4 Cups a Day Caused the Same Hydration As Water.

For nearly a century the conventional advice has been that coffee's caffeine makes it a diuretic and you need a glass of water with every cup. A 2014 University of Birmingham cross-over trial that compared 4 cups of coffee against 4 cups of water across 13 hydration biomarkers found no significant differences on any of them. The morning habit, it turns out, counts as fluid intake — not against it.

By Cyrus Aldine, Nutrition & Physiology · June 11, 2026

Title

Authors
Killer S. C., Blannin A. K., Jeukendrup A. E. (2014)
Institution
University of Birmingham, School of Sport, Exercise and Rehabilitation Sciences
Journal
PLOS ONE, 9(1), e84154
DOI

Sample
50 male habitual coffee drinkers (3–6 cups per day), free-living population, mean age 25 ± 4 years, mean BMI 24 ± 3 kg/m²
Method
Counterbalanced cross-over design, two 3-day trials. Participants consumed 4 × 200 mL of coffee (4 mg/kg caffeine, mean 322 mg) or matched water. Food, fluid, and physical activity were controlled. Washout between arms.

Counterintuition
⚡⚡⚡⚡ 4/5
Replication
Consistent with Grandjean et al. 2000 (J Am Coll Nutr, no diuretic effect at 114–253 mg caffeine in habituated users). Supported by Zhang et al. 2015 systematic review of 16 studies, and Armstrong et al. 2005 and 2007 studies showing no diuretic effect at moderate doses in habitual users. Effect reappears in caffeine-naïve subjects and at higher doses (≥5 mg/kg single dose).

The Coffee-and-Water Paring That Never Made Sense

Walk into any gym or dietitian's office and you'll see the same advice printed as if it were a law of physiology. Coffee is a diuretic, the warning says. Caffeinated drinks don't count toward your daily water intake. Drink a glass of water with every cup. The advice has been repeated so often that few people stop to ask where it came from or whether the studies it cites have anything to do with the way most people actually drink coffee.

The answer to both questions is uncomfortable. The advice traces to a 1928 study by H. B. Eddy and Alice Downs, who showed that 0.5 grams of pure caffeine — roughly 5 espressos consumed in a single sitting by someone whose body was not used to it — produced a measurable spike in urine output. For nearly a century, the field treated that finding as universal. Caffeine dehydrates. Coffee dehydrates. Replace the lost fluid with water.

The problem is the dose. A standard cup of brewed coffee contains 80 to 120 mg of caffeine, not 500. A moderate coffee habit — 3 to 4 cups spread across a day — delivers 240 to 480 mg, with food, in people whose bodies have already adapted to it. The original diuretic claim was tested in caffeine-naive subjects at pharmacological doses. The advice that followed was applied to habitual drinkers at moderate doses. That mismatch is what a 2014 trial at the University of Birmingham set out to test.

A Counterbalanced Cross-Over, Six Days, 13 Biomarkers

Sophie Killer, Andrew Blannin, and Asker Jeukendrup recruited 50 male habitual coffee drinkers who normally consumed 3 to 6 cups per day. Each participant spent three days drinking four 200-mL servings of coffee containing 4 mg per kg of caffeine (averaging 322 mg total) and three days drinking the same volume of water, with the order randomized. Food, physical activity, and other fluid intake were tightly controlled, and plasma caffeine was measured to confirm compliance.

The trial measured hydration across an unusually wide battery of markers. Total body water was calculated before and after each arm via deuterium oxide dilution. Urine was collected over 24 hours and analyzed for volume, specific gravity, osmolality, and creatinine. Standard hematological markers including hematocrit and hemoglobin were drawn daily. Nude body mass was recorded each morning.

The result, in full: there were no significant differences between coffee and water on any of these markers. Total body water was 51.5 ± 1.4 kg on coffee, 51.4 ± 1.3 kg on water. Twenty-four-hour urine volume was 2,409 mL on coffee, 2,428 mL on water — essentially identical. Plasma caffeine measurements confirmed participants actually drank the coffee. The only statistically significant difference was a slightly higher urinary sodium excretion on coffee days (p = 0.02), which the authors interpreted as a small and clinically irrelevant shift in electrolyte handling.

The paper's conclusion was unambiguous: "Coffee, when consumed in moderation by caffeine habituated males, provides similar hydrating qualities to water."

Why the Diuretic Effect Disappeared

The reason comes down to two things: dose and tolerance. Caffeine's diuretic effect is dose-dependent, and it weakens with repeated exposure. Earlier studies cited in the Birmingham paper found that at 4 mg per kg, the diuretic effect appears on the first day only and disappears on subsequent days. The studies that produced robust diuretic effects — including a 1997 German trial and a 2004 Wemple study — used 5 to 8 mg per kg in single doses, often after a 4-to-7-day caffeine washout, on participants who were habitually heavy coffee drinkers forced to abstain. The adenosine receptors that caffeine blocks appear to develop tolerance within days of regular exposure. By the second day of the Birmingham trial, the participants' kidneys had stopped reacting to the caffeine at all from a fluid-balance perspective.

This is also why anecdotally you'll hear "coffee makes me pee a lot" from people who drink it occasionally but not from people who drink it every day. The acute effect is real. The chronic advice is wrong.

A Number Worth Pausing Over

The National Coffee Association estimates that 75% of US adults drink coffee, with an average intake of 3.2 cups per day. The conventional advice to "drink a glass of water with every cup" amounts to roughly 800 mL of extra water per day for a moderate drinker, or about 292 liters per year per person.

Applied to the US adult coffee-drinking population (roughly 190 million adults), the advice suggests an extra 55.5 billion liters of water consumption per year. None of it is medically necessary. Per the Birmingham trial's data, the 800 mL of coffee is hydrating; the advice to add 800 mL of water on top of that is asking people to drink roughly twice the fluid their kidneys need on a typical day. Over years, that pattern contributes to frequent urination, sleep disruption from nocturia, and the perceived urgency that makes people blame coffee itself.

The Strongest Case Against

The Birmingham trial has clear limits, and the strongest counterargument deserves to be stated at full strength.

First, the sample is 50 men. The paper is explicit that it does not test women, and there is published evidence (not cited in this paper) that menstrual cycle phase affects fluid balance. Female habitual coffee drinkers should not assume the result is identical for them.

Second, the dose is moderate: 4 mg per kg. Earlier work cited in the paper's own discussion shows that at 5+ mg per kg, and especially in caffeine-naive participants, the diuretic effect reappears. Heavy occasional coffee drinkers — people who drink a 600 mg energy drink once a week, or who binge coffee on weekends only — may genuinely experience net fluid loss.

Third, the trial is short (three days per arm) and run in free-living conditions, which the authors acknowledge reduces experimental control. They cannot rule out cumulative effects over months or years of moderate intake, though the underlying tolerance mechanism is physiological and well-documented.

Fourth, the 13 biomarkers measured are short-term fluid balance indicators. None of them speak to whether coffee, over decades, has different effects on kidney stone formation, urinary tract infections, or chronic kidney disease. Those are different questions with different evidence bases.

What We Didn't Prove

The Birmingham trial did not test 6 or more cups per day, female habitual drinkers, tea, energy drinks, or other caffeinated beverages. It did not examine long-term cumulative effects — the longest published trial arm is four days. It did not include children, adolescents, or older adults, and it did not address the very different question of whether coffee is good for you. The paper is a hydration study. The cardiovascular, metabolic, sleep, and anxiety effects of chronic caffeine consumption are a separate, contested literature.

It also did not address what happens during heavy sweating. The trial was in free-living conditions but did not include a controlled exercise or heat-stress protocol. Acute fluid loss from exercise is exactly the situation where the moderate-intake result may not apply.

The Bottom Line

For the past century, advice about coffee and hydration has been a textbook case of an acute-pharmacology result being generalized to a chronic-real-world behavior. The original 1928 Eddy-Downs finding was real, but it was a 500-mg single-dose effect in caffeine-naive participants. For habitual coffee drinkers consuming moderate amounts — 3 to 4 cups per day — no hydration deficit has ever been demonstrated in any published trial. The Birmingham study is one well-controlled cross-over of 50 men, but the broader literature converges on the same conclusion. Your morning coffee counts as fluid intake, not against it.

What You Can Do

If you drink 3 to 4 cups of coffee a day and feel fine: keep drinking them, and stop forcing yourself to chug a glass of water after each one for hydration reasons. The extra water will not hurt, but it is a bathroom trip and a swallowed belief, not a medical necessity.

If you are an occasional coffee drinker (a few times a month): yes, you may notice a diuretic effect, and a glass of water afterward is a reasonable hedge. Acute caffeine tolerance is the difference, not the beverage.

If you are a heavy coffee drinker (6 or more cups daily): you are outside what the Birmingham trial tested. The hydration picture is less clear at that dose, and other health concerns (sleep, anxiety, blood pressure) probably deserve more attention than fluid balance.

If you work out in the heat or sweat heavily: rehydration strategies should still emphasize water and electrolytes. Coffee is not a sports drink, and a workout session is exactly the kind of acute fluid loss where the moderate-intake result may not apply.
