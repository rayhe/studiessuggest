Everyone Believes the Oldest Child Is Responsible and the Youngest Is a Rebel. The Largest Study of Birth Order and Personality, 377,000 People, Found the Correlation Is 0.02.

Two independent research groups examined nearly 400,000 people across four countries using both between-family and within-family designs. Birth order showed no meaningful relationship with any Big Five personality trait. The average effect was so small it cannot be detected by any observer, clinician, or parent.

The Family Gospel

Ask any parent of multiple children whether birth position shaped who those children became, and you will get a confident yes. The oldest is responsible, driven, maybe a little bossy; the middle child is diplomatic, overlooked, hungry for attention; the youngest is funny, risk-prone, the family rebel. These labels feel true the way proverbs feel true, carried not by evidence but by the weight of endless repetition.

The idea has serious intellectual roots. Alfred Adler, a contemporary of Freud, built an entire theory of personality around sibling position in the 1920s, and his framework became a fixture of introductory psychology courses worldwide. Frank Sulloway's 1996 book Born to Rebel spent 25 years marshaling historical evidence that laterborns drove scientific revolutions while firstborns defended the status quo, earning praise from the New York Times as "daring...a stunning achievement" and becoming the scaffolding on which millions of people hung their self-understanding.

Then two research groups, working independently, ran the numbers on sample sizes large enough to settle the question.

What the Data Actually Show

In 2015, Julia Rohrer and colleagues at the University of Leipzig examined data from three national panel studies covering 20,186 adults across the United States, the United Kingdom, and Germany. They used both between-family comparisons, in which people from different families are measured against each other, and within-family comparisons, in which siblings are compared directly within the same household. They tested all five major personality traits: extraversion, emotional stability, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness to experience.

Birth order showed no effect on any of them. Zero. Not a small effect, not a debatable one, but a genuine null result. The only exception was a slight decline in self-reported intellect with later birth positions, amounting to about a tenth of a standard deviation, but on the traits people actually care about when they invoke "the responsible one" or "the rebel," the researchers found nothing at all.

That same year, Rodica Damian and Brent Roberts at the University of Illinois published what remains the largest birth-order-and-personality study ever conducted, drawing on 377,000 American high school students from the Project Talent dataset while controlling for gender, age, family size, socioeconomic status, and family structure. The average correlation between birth order and any personality trait was 0.02, a number Roberts described bluntly: "You are not going to be able to see it with the naked eye. You're not going to be able to sit two people down next to each other and see the differences between them. It's not noticeable by anybody."

What 0.02 Actually Means

A correlation of 0.02 is not merely small in the way that many psychological effects are small. Squaring it yields r² = 0.0004, meaning birth order explains four hundredths of one percent of the variation in personality, while the remaining 99.96% of what makes a person extraverted, conscientious, or neurotic comes from somewhere else entirely.

For scale, consider that the correlation between household income and SAT scores, a relationship that has driven decades of education policy debate, sits at roughly 0.40, making it twenty times larger than the birth-order effect. If you took 100 pairs of firstborns and laterborns and asked a clinical psychologist with unlimited time and full interview access to guess which member of each pair was the firstborn, they would get roughly 51 right, barely distinguishable from flipping a coin.

The Strongest Case for Birth Order

The best counterargument comes from Sulloway himself, and it deserves to be heard at full strength. His Born to Rebel examined historical figures' responses to scientific revolutions, not questionnaire scores collected in a university lab, and his claim was never that firstborns score slightly higher on conscientiousness inventories administered under fluorescent lighting. It was that the family ecosystem creates adaptive niches visible only in high-stakes, real-world contexts: who embraces a revolution and who fights it, who takes risks when something genuinely matters and who plays it safe. A 2014 Italian study of 96 families found that firstborns scored measurably higher on conservatism values, consistent with the niche theory, and the intelligence finding that firstborns enjoy a roughly 1-IQ-point advantage has replicated consistently across studies and decades.

Personality questionnaires capture self-reported tendencies in ordinary circumstances and may genuinely miss the way birth order shapes behavior under pressure, in competitive environments, or when family legacy is at stake, leaving open the possibility that birth order affects specific behavioral patterns that broad-spectrum instruments cannot detect. Maybe the effect hides in situations no questionnaire measures.

Rohrer's team tested that possibility directly in a 2017 follow-up published in Psychological Science, applying specification-curve analysis to dozens of narrow personality traits rather than just the Big Five composites, and they found no effects on any of them. The niche theory's escape hatch appears sealed by the same group that sealed the broader case, which means the most sophisticated version of the birth-order hypothesis now lacks empirical support at both the broad trait level and the granular level of specific behaviors and attitudes its defenders claimed would be the right place to look.

What We Didn't Prove

All three national panels in the Rohrer study relied on self-report personality measures, and people may lack insight into their own traits in ways that could mask effects visible in observed behavior but invisible in self-assessment questionnaires.

The Damian and Roberts sample, while enormous, used personality measures developed in 1960 on an ad hoc basis rather than validated Big Five instruments. Cross-cultural evidence remains concentrated in Western nations, and effects might emerge in cultures with stronger sibling hierarchies, particularly in parts of East Asia where firstborn sons carry explicit family obligations that go well beyond anything a personality questionnaire can capture.

There is also a confound that no study, however large, can fully eliminate: birth order correlates with family size, which correlates with socioeconomic status, which correlates with parenting style, creating a chain of associations that statistical controls reduce but cannot erase.

The Bottom Line

Birth order is a story we tell about ourselves that has almost no empirical support. The combined weight of nearly 400,000 participants across multiple countries and two independent research designs converges on the same conclusion: being born first, middle, or last has no detectable impact on whether a person becomes outgoing or withdrawn, agreeable or combative, disciplined or freewheeling. The myth persists because parents observe their conscientious 12-year-old and their rebellious 8-year-old and attribute the difference to birth position, but conscientiousness increases reliably with age, meaning a 12-year-old will almost always appear more responsible than her 8-year-old sibling not because she's the firstborn, but simply because she has had four more years to develop.

What You Can Do

Stop adjusting expectations for children based on where they fall in the birth sequence, because the data say it predicts personality no better than flipping a coin.

If you catch yourself thinking "that's such a middle child thing," pause and consider that you are applying a framework with an r² of 0.0004 to a human being.

Evaluate job candidates, romantic partners, and friends on what they actually do; the hiring manager who asks "are you a firstborn?" is operating on the same evidentiary footing as one who checks a zodiac sign.
