The "Love Hormone" Won't Make You Trust a Stranger. A Registered Replication Found No Effect, Except in Men Who Trust No One.

In 2005, 58 men and a nasal spray launched the idea that oxytocin makes us trust each other. A preregistered replication with over 95% power found nothing, and a 2026 pooled analysis of 532 participants says any general effect is too small to matter. The one place it still shows up: men who start out deeply distrustful.

By Maya Chen, Neuroscience & Behavior · November 22, 2026

The Spray That Launched a Thousand Headlines

Ask a stranger what oxytocin does. The answer comes back fast: the love hormone, the cuddle chemical, the molecule that makes people trust each other. TED talks repeat it, and a small industry sells nasal spray as liquid trust. It traces back to one experiment published in Nature in June 2005, an experiment with 58 participants.

The paper's title left no room for doubt, "Oxytocin increases trust in humans," and its authors reported that men who sniffed the hormone handed over more money to strangers in an investment game, a result that within a few years had earned more than 5,000 citations. What almost nobody noticed was how thin the evidence was.

Fifty-Eight Men and a Money Game

Michael Kosfeld and his colleagues at the University of Zurich recruited 58 healthy young men, split them in two, gave one group 24 international units of oxytocin through a nasal spray and the other a placebo, kept everyone blind, and about fifty minutes later had each man play investor, free to hand up to 12 monetary units to an anonymous trustee, knowing the money would triple and the trustee could return what he liked, so the full amount meant complete trust.

The oxytocin group handed over more: transfers averaged 17 percent higher, 45 percent entrusted the maximum amount against 21 percent on placebo, and the median transfer was 10 monetary units against 8. A second experiment with 61 different men replaced the human trustee with a computer that returned money at random, and there the groups behaved identically, which the authors read as proof the effect was social, not risk appetite.

The Arithmetic Nobody Reported

Look closer and the foundation looks narrow, because the headline result rested on a p-value of 0.029, computed one-tailed, and run the same test two-tailed, the standard most journals expect, the value is 0.058, above the usual 0.05 line, which means the paper that launched a field would not have cleared the bar today. The effect size, about half a standard deviation, was large for one nasal spray, and large effects from tiny samples are the ones most likely to shrink on a second try.

Then there is the file drawer: one laboratory in Belgium, led by Moïra Mikolajczak, decided in 2016 to publish everything it had tried, and across eight studies, 25 tasks, and 453 participants, only one task produced a main effect, and the arithmetic is plain: run 25 tests and, by chance alone at the usual 5 percent threshold, you expect about 1.25 false positives, and the lab found exactly one. Its published record suggested a field full of discoveries. Its complete record suggested noise. A 2016 analysis agreed: the typical study in healthy adults had about 16 percent statistical power, against the 80 percent benchmark, and the median clinical trial had 12 percent. At that power, most published positive findings were probably not true effects.

The Replication That Was Built to End the Argument

The decisive test came in 2020, when Carolyn Declerck and colleagues, including Ernst Fehr himself, ran a preregistered replication in Nature Human Behaviour. They rebuilt the original conditions faithfully, including five minutes of face-to-face contact later studies had dropped, powered the study above 95 percent to detect the original effect, and found nothing: oxytocin did not increase trusting behavior in the minimal-social-contact condition that had produced the 2005 result.

A 2026 registered report in Cortex went further, with Charlotte Kroll and colleagues running a fresh replication of 211 participants, pooling it with Declerck's data for 532 in total, and asking a stricter question: is the effect big enough to care about? Equivalence testing put it inside a minimal range. The general claim had failed twice, in the two largest tests ever run.

The Strongest Counterargument

That is not the end of the story, and the strongest reply comes from the same group that ran the failed replication. In August 2026, Declerck, Vogt, and Fehr published a preregistered study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences that asked a narrower question: what if you test only men who score low on dispositional trust? They recruited 359 such men, ran the trust game anonymously, and found oxytocin raised trusting behavior by roughly 15 percent, rising to 16.9 percent when pooled with 219 low-trusting men from an earlier sample. Their reading: oxytocin is not a blanket prosocial factor, but a lift for trust where trust starts low.

There are other reasons to keep the door open. Oxytocin's best-established roles, in childbirth, breastfeeding, and bonding, involve the body's own supply, released in pulses over months, not a single squirt up the nose. How much of a nasal dose reaches the brain is disputed; one 2016 review argued very little reaches the cerebrospinal fluid. The social salience hypothesis offers another frame, in which oxytocin amplifies social signals, friendly or threatening, which explains why results flip with context, while the 2026 Cortex pooling found no sign that baseline trust changes the effect, a result that sits in tension with the PNAS finding and leaves the subgroup story unsettled. None of this rescues the original claim; it only narrows it: the universal version is the part that failed.

What We Didn’t Prove

Every study in this story tested men, almost all young, healthy, and in their twenties, so nothing here speaks about women, older adults, or anyone outside a laboratory trust game. The trust game measures one behavior, handing money to a stranger once, and risking cash is not the same as trusting a partner, a doctor, or a government. The sprays sold online are unregulated and unstandardized, none of this research supports using them, and the 2026 subgroup finding comes from one preregistered study plus a pooled sample. It has not yet been independently replicated. The honest summary is smaller than the legend: a nasal spray does not make people trust each other.

The Bottom Line

A 2005 Nature study with 58 men turned oxytocin into the love hormone, yet a preregistered replication with over 95 percent power found no effect, and a 2026 pooled analysis of 532 participants found any general effect too small to matter. The exception is men who start out deeply distrustful: a 2026 study found a 15 percent lift. Oxytocin is not a trust spray.

What You Can Do

Do not buy oxytocin nasal spray to become more trusting, because the products sold online are unregulated, the evidence for a general effect has collapsed, and no dose has a demonstrated benefit for healthy adults. If you find it hard to trust people, the evidence points the other way: trust is built by repeated, low-stakes cooperation, keeping small promises, and noticing follow-through, not by chemistry. When a headline credits one hormone with a complex behavior, check three things: sample size, replication, and whether the replication was preregistered. A 58-person study with a one-tailed p-value is a starting point, not a conclusion. And if a finding sounds too neat to be true, ask who profits, because here the answer was a small industry of spray bottles.
