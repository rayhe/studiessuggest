Arguing Raises Your Risk of Catching a Cold. Hugs Erase the Effect, a 404-Person Viral-Challenge Study Found

Researchers phoned 404 healthy adults for 14 evenings, tallying their fights and their hugs, then dripped rhinovirus into their noses in quarantine. When conflict was frequent, infection risk climbed steeply for the rarely hugged and stayed flat for the often hugged.

The Advice Nobody Gives You at Flu Season

Cold season brings the same advice every year: wash your hands, sleep eight hours, take vitamin C, eat chicken soup, and avoid sick strangers. No doctor has ever added "get hugged more" to the list, yet a 2015 study from Carnegie Mellon suggests the hug belongs on the list anyway, with a twist: hugs helped the people going through conflict most, by erasing the damage the conflict did.

The idea that stress makes you catch colds is older than this study. Sheldon Cohen had shown it twice before, first in a 1991 experiment in the New England Journal of Medicine, then in a 1997 JAMA study on social ties and colds. Nobody had tested the mechanism: social support is a vague feeling, and Cohen wanted to know whether a specific physical behavior could carry that protection, so he picked the most ordinary one available: being hugged.

Fourteen Evenings of Phone Calls, Then a Virus Up the Nose

Cohen's team recruited 404 healthy adults in Pittsburgh, ages 18 to 55, and phoned each of them on 14 consecutive evenings with the same two questions: did you have any tension or conflict with someone today, and did anyone hug you today? Hugs were reported on a median 68 percent of days, conflict on 7 percent, so hugs outnumbered fights by nearly ten to one.

Then came the part no survey can fake. One to three weeks after the interviews, each volunteer checked into a hotel floor set aside for quarantine and received nasal drops containing rhinovirus 39 or influenza A, at doses chosen to maximize infection. For five or six days, researchers tracked mucus and congestion from nasal washes and weighed tissues, and infection was confirmed by recovering the virus from a nasal wash or by a fourfold rise in antibodies a month later. The virus did its work. 315 of the 404 became infected, 78 percent, and 127 met the criteria for a clinical cold, 31 percent.

The Arithmetic of a Hug

Across the full range of hug frequency, the odds of infection fell by 61 percent, an odds ratio of 0.39 with a confidence interval of 0.16 to 0.96, the main effect, real but fragile. The stronger result is the interaction, the one the study was designed to find: among people who were hugged rarely, more frequent conflict meant a steeply rising risk of infection, a clear rise at p=.008, while among people who were hugged often, the same conflicts did nothing at all, the slope flat at p=.17 and the interaction significant at p=.005.

Arguing made you more likely to catch the cold the researchers gave you, unless hugs were a regular part of your days, in which case arguing stopped mattering. Support showed the same pattern, and hugs accounted for 32 percent of support's protective effect. Among the 315 who did get infected, hugs kept working: people who had been hugged more often cleared their nasal passages faster, beta=-0.13, p=.044, though hugs explained only 1.3 percent of the variance in clearance time, and hugs did not change mucus production. The pattern held after controlling for personality and for both viruses. Hugs on ordinary, conflict-free days mattered at least as much as hugs on tense days, which suggests regular hugging does the work. Only 127 of the 315 infected, 40 percent, developed a full cold.

The Strongest Counterargument

Not everyone buys it. Tiffany Field, of the Touch Research Institute, told the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette that she disagreed with the conclusion, because in her view the evidence did not show that stress caused infection, or that hugging helped much at all. She added a warning that cuts the other way: hugging people during flu season increases your exposure to their germs, and if the hugger is sick, the hug is a transmission event, not a shield.

The paper hands its critics three more. First, nobody was randomly assigned to be hugged: hugs were observed, not prescribed, so frequently hugged people may differ from rarely hugged people in ways the study never measured. Personality and ten covariates were controlled, but residual confounding is the permanent tax on this kind of design. Second, the clean 61 percent figure depends on the model, because once the researchers adjusted for how often participants interacted with other people at all, a control for simply being around people, the main effect of hugs on infection faded to an odds ratio of 0.57 and lost significance at p=.21, though the interaction survived that adjustment. Third, and most awkward: hugs had no effect on developing a clinical cold, with an odds ratio of 1.02 and p=.96, so infection moved, and one objective sign of illness moved, but the cold itself did not.

The replication gap is real. No one has repeated the viral-challenge design with hugs, while the mechanism has support from the lab, where partner touch blunted cortisol and heart rate responses to stress in 2007, and where a warm-touch intervention raised oxytocin and lowered blood pressure in 2008. But those studies test physiology, not colds, and the Zurich study tested women only, so the hug finding rests on one team, one design, and one dataset.

What We Didn’t Prove

This study tested healthy adults between 18 and 55, so it says nothing about children, older adults, or anyone immunocompromised, and it never recorded who did the hugging or who the conflict was with, which means a hug from a spouse and a hug from a coworker count the same. Hugs were self-reported in nightly phone calls, which invites memory slips, and the viral dose was chosen to maximize infection in people selected for low prior immunity, so the 78 percent infection rate is a laboratory artifact, not a picture of an ordinary winter, and the study also cannot say whether the touch itself does the work or whether hugs simply signal a close relationship, while the clinical cold outcome, the thing people actually care about, did not move.

The Bottom Line

Fights make you more likely to catch a cold, but only if hugs are rare. In 404 adults deliberately exposed to a cold virus, frequent conflict raised infection risk among the rarely hugged and left the often hugged untouched. Hugs are not a vaccine, but they erased the stress penalty on infection. A hug costs nothing, and the evidence says it counts for more than comfort.

What You Can Do

If you are going through a tense stretch, an argument at home or a conflict at work, seek out a hug from someone close. What mattered was frequency across two weeks, not one dramatic embrace, so treat it as a habit. Do not go hugging strangers or sick people to boost your immunity, because close contact with an infected person is how viruses travel. If you rarely get hugged, perceived support protected people too, so a close friend who listens and a community you belong to sit on the same pathway. Keep the basics in place, because sleep, handwashing, and staying home when you are sick do more than any single social gesture.
