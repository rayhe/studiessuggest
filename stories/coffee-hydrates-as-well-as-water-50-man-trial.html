Coffee Doesn't Dehydrate You. In 50 Men, Four Cups a Day Hydrated as Well as Water

For decades, coffee drinkers have been told to chase every cup with a glass of water or the caffeine would dry them out. A team at the University of Birmingham gave 50 men either four cups of coffee or the same volume of water for three days and measured their total body water with the gold-standard method. The coffee drinkers were no drier.

By Priya Natarajan, Nutrition & Food Science · November 11, 2026

The Rule Everyone Follows

Order a coffee and someone will eventually tell you to drink a glass of water with it. Caffeine makes you urinate, the reasoning goes, so every cup leaves you a little drier than before, and the only fix is to replace what the coffee supposedly stole. Some guidelines once told people to leave caffeinated drinks out of daily fluid totals entirely.

The logic has a gap. A cup of black coffee is more than 98 percent water, so for the drink to dehydrate you, the caffeine would have to push out more fluid than the cup puts in, and that is a claim about amounts, not about whether caffeine is a diuretic at all. Caffeine does make the kidneys excrete more sodium and water, so the question is whether that effect survives the arithmetic of the cup in people adapted to caffeine over years of daily drinking.

Four Cups Against Four Cups of Water

Sophie Killer and her colleagues at the University of Birmingham decided to test the claim directly, recruiting 52 healthy men aged 18 to 46, all drinking three to six cups a day, and putting each man through two trials. In one trial he drank four 200-millilitre mugs of black instant coffee a day, dosed to deliver 4 milligrams of caffeine per kilogram of body weight; in the other, four identical mugs of plain water. Each trial ran for three days, with a ten-day washout between them, and the order was counterbalanced so half the men started on coffee and half on water.

The controls were tight. Diet was provided and identical in both trials, alcohol was banned, and exercise was limited to walking. Every morning the men reported to the laboratory fasted, gave blood, and were weighed nude, while total body water, the gold-standard measure of hydration, was tracked with deuterium oxide dilution, which labels body water, and urine was collected over 24 hours and tested for volume, concentration, osmolality, and creatinine, with plasma caffeine confirming compliance. Two of the 52 men dropped out, leaving 50 who completed both trials.

The Numbers Came Back Identical

Total body water did not move. It averaged 51.5 kilograms on the coffee trial and 51.4 on the water trial, a 100-gram gap in a body carrying more than 50 kilograms of water, roughly a stick of butter. Twenty-four-hour urine volume came out at 2,409 millilitres on coffee and 2,428 on water, a difference of 19 millilitres, less than half a shot glass, in more than two litres of daily output, while blood and urine markers showed no difference between the two conditions.

One number did move. Urinary sodium excretion was higher on coffee, the fingerprint of the diuretic effect everyone worries about, though it did not add up to dehydration. Body mass drifted down slightly in both trials, by about 0.4 kilograms over the three days, which points to the controlled diet, not the coffee.

The myth ignores the paper's own figure: about 1.6 billion cups of coffee are drunk worldwide every day. At 200 millilitres a cup, that is 320 million litres a day the dehydration rule writes off as a net loss. Four cups, the dose in the trial, is 800 millilitres, nearly a third of the 2.5 litres a day the European Food Safety Authority lists as an adequate water intake for men, while the trial dose of about 300 milligrams of caffeine for a 75-kilogram adult sits below the 400 milligrams a day the US Food and Drug Administration cites as generally safe and well below the 500-milligram doses where caffeine's diuretic effect shows up clearly.

The Strongest Counterargument

The strongest case against this result starts with who paid for it: the study was funded by the Institute for Scientific Information on Coffee, a coffee-industry research nonprofit, and one author, Asker Jeukendrup, was employed by PepsiCo at the time. The paper states the funders had no role in design, analysis, or publication, but industry funding is a real reason to want independent confirmation, which this trial has never had on total body water.

The population is narrow. Fifty men, all of them already drinking three to six cups a day, all young, and no women, because the menstrual cycle disturbs fluid-balance measurements. Caffeine tolerance is the hinge of the whole result, since the literature shows tolerance to the diuretic effect builds with regular use and fades after as little as four days without caffeine. A person who almost never drinks coffee and then downs a large dose is a different case, and this trial says nothing about them.

The boundary condition has its own study. In 1997, Neuhäuser-Berthold and colleagues gave habitual coffee drinkers six cups a day, 624 milligrams of caffeine, after five days of caffeine deprivation, and found total body water fell 2.7 percent while urine output rose 41 percent. The dose was higher, the drinkers had lost their tolerance, and the result flipped. A 2017 meta-analysis agreed: coffee with high caffeine content increased fluid and electrolyte excretion, while low-caffeine coffee did not. The honest reading is not that caffeine has no diuretic effect, but that at the moderate doses regular drinkers actually consume, the water in the cup wins.

The design has another limit: three days per trial is long enough to catch acute dehydration and short enough to miss slow drift, and the trial tested black instant coffee, not the sugary, milky, or energy-drink versions of caffeine people actually argue about. The result covers moderate black coffee in habituated drinkers, not a litre of energy drink.

What We Didn’t Prove

This was one small trial, 50 men, at one university, that has not been directly replicated and did not test women, older adults, people with kidney disease or heart failure, or anyone taking diuretic medication, nor decaffeinated coffee, very high doses, or caffeine consumed as pills or energy drinks. It cannot prove coffee hydrates everyone, and it says nothing about coffee's other effects on sleep or blood pressure. The sodium finding is a reminder that the diuretic mechanism is real even when the net effect is zero.

The Bottom Line

Four cups of coffee a day hydrated 50 regular coffee drinkers as well as four cups of water, with total body water, urine volume, and blood markers indistinguishable between the two. The diuretic effect of caffeine is real but small at moderate doses, and in people who drink coffee every day the water in the cup more than covers it, so count your morning coffee toward your fluids. Keep drinking water too, because coffee is not a substitute for it, but stop treating the cup as a debt you owe your body.

What You Can Do

If you drink coffee every day, count it toward your fluid intake: four cups is roughly 800 millilitres, nearly a third of a typical daily target. Keep your total caffeine under about 400 milligrams a day, the level regulators cite as generally safe, and spread it out rather than taking a large dose at once. If you rarely drink caffeine, expect the first cups to send you to the bathroom more often. If you take diuretic medication or have kidney or heart disease, ask your clinician first, since this trial studied healthy young men.
