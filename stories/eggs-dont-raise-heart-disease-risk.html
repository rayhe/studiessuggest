An Egg a Day Didn’t Raise Heart Risk. In 215,618 People Followed for 32 Years, the Yolk Was Innocent and the Bacon Was the Problem

Harvard researchers followed 215,618 health professionals for up to 32 years and pooled 1.7 million people in a meta-analysis. An egg a day carried no detectable heart risk. Swapping it for bacon or sausage raised risk 15%.

By Sophie Reiner, Nutrition Science · November 28, 2026

The Villain of the Breakfast Table

For half a century, the egg sat at the center of breakfast guilt. In 1968 the American Heart Association told Americans to eat no more than three eggs a week. Federal guidance capped dietary cholesterol at 300 milligrams a day, and a single large egg carries about 186 of them, so one yolk used up more than half the daily allowance before the toast was buttered. Egg-white omelettes became the virtuous choice, the yolk went in the bin, and nobody asked whether the bin was the right place for it.

The logic felt airtight: eggs are the most concentrated source of cholesterol in the ordinary diet, cholesterol clogs arteries, and therefore eggs clog arteries. The chain had one weak link: the middle step was never as solid as it looked. Feeding studies do show dietary cholesterol nudging blood cholesterol upward, but the effect is modest and varies wildly between people, the body makes most of its own cholesterol anyway, and what nobody had done was follow enough egg eaters for long enough, with diet measured repeatedly, to see whether the yolks actually translated into heart attacks.

The Biggest Test Ever Run

A team at the Harvard Chan School of Public Health did it. They pooled three cohorts of American health professionals: 83,349 nurses, 90,214 younger nurses, and 42,055 male health professionals, 215,618 people in all, and followed them for up to 32 years. Diet was reassessed every two to four years with food-frequency questionnaires validated against weighed diet records. Over 5.54 million person-years, 14,806 participants had a heart attack, died of coronary disease, or had a stroke, each case confirmed from medical records by physicians who did not know what the person ate.

The researchers also pooled the world’s evidence, gathering 28 prospective cohort studies, 33 risk estimates, 1,720,108 participants, and 139,195 cardiovascular events, and re-analyzing them together. It was an update of a 1999 study by the same Harvard group, now with 24 more years of follow-up and more than ten times the events.

The Result Was Nothing

People who ate at least one egg a day had no higher risk of cardiovascular disease than people who ate less than one egg a month. The hazard ratio was 0.93, with a confidence interval from 0.82 to 1.05, so the best estimate is no effect at all. The meta-analysis agreed: each additional egg a day carried a pooled relative risk of 0.98, confidence interval 0.93 to 1.03.

Geography added a twist: in Asian cohorts, an egg a day was linked to slightly lower risk, a relative risk of 0.92. In American cohorts the figure was 1.01, in European cohorts 1.05, both statistically indistinguishable from 1.00. The authors also calculated that an unmeasured confounder would need a risk ratio of 1.43 with both egg eating and heart disease to turn the null result positive.

The Swap That Actually Hurts

The study’s most useful finding was not about eggs at all. The researchers modeled what happens when one egg a day is replaced by one serving of something else. Replace it with processed red meat, bacon or sausage, and cardiovascular risk rose 15 percent, while unprocessed red meat raised it 10 percent, full-fat milk 11 percent, and replacing the egg with fish, poultry, legumes, nuts, whole grains, or yogurt changed nothing at all. The bacon was the problem.

That inversion is the story, because the breakfast advice of the last fifty years pushed people away from the egg and toward the foods that sit beside it on the plate, and here is what that trade looks like at population scale. The cohorts recorded 267 cardiovascular events per 100,000 person-years, so a 15 percent increase means about 40 extra events per 100,000 people each year. If all 260 million American adults swapped one egg a day for bacon or sausage, the arithmetic implies roughly 104,000 extra cardiovascular events a year, though that is a back-of-envelope calculation rather than a measured fact, it assumes the cohorts’ rates apply to the whole country, and since the cohorts were unusually healthy professionals, the true figure could be higher rather than lower.

The Strongest Counterargument

The case against this result is real, and it starts with a rival study. Zhong and colleagues, writing in JAMA in 2019, pooled 29,615 Americans from six cohorts and found each additional half egg a day linked to a 6 percent higher risk of cardiovascular disease, and the biology supports them, since controlled feeding trials show dietary cholesterol raises LDL cholesterol and eggs are its richest source. The Harvard result is observational, and the people who ate the most eggs were also heavier, smoked more, and ate more red and processed meat. Statistical adjustment can only do so much with that pattern.

There are two more holes, starting with how few people ate more than one egg a day: in 1998-99, only 1.24 percent did. The study says little about two or three eggs daily. And the diabetes subgroup is unresolved: the meta-analysis found a relative risk of 1.25 per egg a day in people with type 2 diabetes, though the confidence interval just crossed 1.00 and the signal did not appear in the three Harvard cohorts themselves. No randomized trial has ever tested eggs against hard cardiovascular endpoints, so “no association found” is not “proven safe.”

What We Didn’t Prove

This study cannot prove eggs are harmless. It is observational, diet was self-reported, and the participants were mostly white American health professionals, a group that eats better and lives longer than the average American, while the meta-analysis was heterogeneous, with an I-squared of 62 percent, meaning the studies disagreed with each other more than chance would predict. The cohorts were funded by the National Institutes of Health, and while the lead author disclosed speaking fees from Dairy Farmers of Canada outside this work, no author reported funding from the egg industry. The finding covers up to one egg a day, and beyond that the evidence thins fast.

The Bottom Line

The egg was convicted on a theory, not on evidence. The yolk was innocent. The largest study ever run on the question, backed by a meta-analysis of 1.7 million people, found no heart risk from an egg a day, and found that the food people swapped in instead, bacon and sausage, carries the risk the egg was blamed for. That does not make eggs a health food. It makes them a normal food, and it makes the yolk you have been throwing away a casualty of advice that was never tested.

What You Can Do

If you eat an egg a day, the evidence says you can stop worrying about it. If you replaced eggs with bacon or sausage because you thought you were being careful, that swap is the one worth reversing. Keep the rest of the plate in view: the people in these cohorts who ate eggs alongside vegetables, whole grains, and fish did fine, and the risk sat with the processed meat, not the egg. If you have type 2 diabetes, treat this as unsettled and talk to your clinician, since the one worrying signal in the data sits with that group, and if you eat two or three eggs a day, know that this study barely tested that habit, because the evidence covers one egg, not a stack of them.
