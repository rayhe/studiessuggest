They Were Paid $102 to Quit Facebook for a Month. They Came Out Happier, Less Informed, and Still Using It Less Weeks Later

Economists asked 2,743 Facebook users what it would take to log off for four weeks. The people who did came back happier and less polarized, scored worse on a news quiz, and kept using the site less afterward, a trade-off that a 2025 meta-analysis says may not hold for shorter breaks.

By Theo Baxter, Behavioral Economics · November 16, 2026

What Would It Take to Log Off?

Ask people whether Facebook is good for them and you get two answers: poison, or a free utility for keeping up with family. Both camps were guessing, because almost nobody had run the obvious test: pay people to stop, at random, and measure what changes.

Four economists ran it in the fall of 2018. Hunt Allcott, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer, and Matthew Gentzkow recruited 2,743 Facebook users and asked each the smallest sum they would accept to deactivate for four weeks, ending just after the November 6 midterm election. Sixty-one percent named a price under $102, so the researchers offered exactly that to a randomly chosen half and left the rest alone.

That price is the first finding. Facebook is free, yet the average user in the study charged about $3.64 a day for access, roughly $1,224 a year, and only from the people cheap enough to accept the offer. People guard the service as if it carried a subscription, and then, as the experiment showed, they feel better without it.

An Hour a Day, Returned

Deactivation freed about sixty minutes a day, 28 hours across four weeks, and the recipients spent it the way people spend found time: watching television alone and socializing with family and friends. Nobody took up the violin. The hour went to ordinary life, and ordinary life scored better.

On a combined index of happiness, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety, well-being rose by 0.09 standard deviations, which sounds small until you set it against a yardstick: in the team's later experiment, the average psychological intervention, things like therapy and mindfulness training, produced 0.27 standard deviations across 419 trials, so a month off Facebook delivered about one-third of a therapy effect from doing nothing except not opening an app.

The gains came with a cost the same numbers make plain. News knowledge fell by 0.19 standard deviations, news consumption dropped about eight minutes a day, and political polarization fell too. The knowledge loss was about twice the well-being gain, 0.19 divided by 0.09, and the people who quit were calmer, happier, and less sure who was running for Congress.

The most revealing result came afterward. A month later the paid quitters were still using the Facebook mobile app about twelve minutes less per day, and the price they named to quit again had fallen. If that cut held for a year, it would free about 73 hours. They had been guarding something that made them worse off, and a month away taught them the guarding was the habit, not the value.

The Bigger, Fresher Test

The 2018 study was one election, one platform, and one research team, so in November 2026 the same lead authors published a far bigger version in the American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, run before the 2020 election with 31,282 participants, in which people paid to deactivate Facebook for six weeks scored 0.060 standard deviations better on a mood index than controls who quit for only the first week, while Instagram showed a smaller gain of 0.041 that did not clear the authors' own significance bar once adjusted for multiple tests, landing at p = 0.14. Fewer than one percent of invited people finished, and the sample leaned more liberal and civically engaged than the platforms' users. Small, uneven, hard to generalize, but the direction matched.

Two earlier studies point the same way. In Denmark, 1,095 people randomly assigned to quit Facebook for one week reported life satisfaction of 8.11 against 7.74 on a ten-point scale, with 88 percent of quitters calling themselves happy against 81 percent of controls, and the biggest gains among heavy users and enviers. Separately, Braghieri, Levy, and Makarin found Facebook's staggered rollout across American colleges worsened students' mental health, especially depression.

The Strongest Counterargument

The most serious challenge comes from a preregistered meta-analysis published in March 2025. Laura Lemahieu and colleagues pooled ten abstinence experiments covering 4,674 adults and 38 effect sizes, and found no significant effect on positive affect, negative affect, or life satisfaction. The pooled numbers were essentially zero: g = 0.03, g = -0.01, and g = 0.03. Their conclusion was blunt: stepping away may not be the best way to improve well-being.

That null result deserves its full weight, and it has an explanation that cuts both ways: most breaks in the meta-analysis lasted a week or less, most studies were small, with little power and poor compliance, and in one study only 13.7 percent of participants actually stayed off social media, while Allcott's four-week deactivation is the longest and largest experiment in the pool and the 2026 follow-up ran six weeks, so a week of white-knuckling may do nothing while a month of actually being gone does something real. The meta-analysis also cannot rule out small effects: its own authors note low power to detect them.

A second challenge is that nobody was blinded. Participants knew they were being paid to quit, and people who expect to feel better often report feeling better. The 2026 authors say so directly: demand effects cannot be fully ruled out. A third is selection: only people willing to quit cheaply were randomized in 2018, and under one percent of invitees finished the 2020 study. And the raw correlation between heavy use and low well-being was about three times the causal effect, so most coverage overstates the harm by roughly a factor of three.

What We Didn’t Prove

This was American adults, using Facebook, in 2018, for four weeks, with self-reported outcomes and no blinding. It does not prove that quitting TikTok helps teenagers, that a week off Instagram does anything, or that Facebook causes clinical depression. The well-being gain is an average: some participants surely felt worse, cut off from groups and events that lived on the platform. The 2025 meta-analysis says the average effect across shorter breaks is indistinguishable from zero, and the 2026 follow-up says the Instagram effect may be nothing at all after adjusting for multiple tests. The honest summary is narrower than the slogan: a month away from Facebook, with the account deactivated, made the average adult here a little happier, a little calmer, and a little less informed.

The Bottom Line

People charged $102 to give up a free service for four weeks, then came out happier by 0.09 standard deviations, less informed by 0.19, and still using the app less a month later. The knowledge lost was about twice the happiness gained, and the gain was about one-third of what therapy delivers. A month off Facebook is not a cure and not a catastrophe. It is a trade: 28 hours returned, one-third of a therapy effect gained, and a measurable slice of the news surrendered.

What You Can Do

Try four weeks, not one. The two big trials ran four and six weeks, while the meta-analysis found most one-week breaks did nothing, so a weekend detox is the version least likely to work. Delete the app from your phone. Decide in advance what the freed hour is for, because an unassigned hour gets refilled. Keep a separate news source, since the knowledge loss was twice the mood gain. And when the month ends, re-price the habit before you go back: ask what you would accept to quit again, and if the number fell, you were overvaluing the service.
