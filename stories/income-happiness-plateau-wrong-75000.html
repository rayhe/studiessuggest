Money Keeps Buying Happiness Past $75,000. The Famous Plateau Holds Only for the Unhappiest Fifth.

Two rival researchers teamed up to reanalyze 33,391 Americans, and the $75,000 happiness plateau turned out to be real only for the least happy fifth. For everyone else, happiness keeps rising with income, though by less than the folklore promises.

The Number Everyone Knows

Ask almost anyone how much money it takes to be happy, and you will get a number: $75,000 a year. The figure comes from a 2010 paper by Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton, both Nobel laureates, who analyzed more than 450,000 responses to Gallup’s daily survey and found that day-to-day emotional well-being rose with income, then stopped, while life satisfaction kept climbing and happiness hit a ceiling at $75,000. The number escaped the journals and became folk wisdom.

Eleven years later, a second paper in the same journal said the ceiling did not exist: Matthew Killingsworth, a researcher at the University of Pennsylvania, had built a phone app that pinged 33,391 working adults three times a day to ask how they felt right now. Across 1,725,994 momentary reports, happiness kept rising with income well past $200,000. Two careful studies. Same journal. Opposite answers.

Rivals, Not Referees

Instead of trading rebuttals, Killingsworth and Kahneman reanalyzed the data together, with psychologist Barbara Mellers as referee, in what Kahneman called adversarial collaboration. The deal was explicit: find where the two results came from, not which side won. Deaton did not take part, and the paper says not to read it as his endorsement. Their joint paper appeared in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in March 2023.

The mistake was in the average: both earlier studies had asked what happens to the average person as income rises, while the joint team asked what happens to people at different levels of happiness, lining people up inside each income band, from least to most happy, and tracking the 15th, 30th, 50th, 70th, and 85th percentiles separately, comparing slopes below and above $100,000, the old $90,000 threshold adjusted for inflation.

Three different patterns emerged: for the least happy 15 to 20 percent, the plateau is real: happiness climbs steeply to about $100,000, then barely moves, the slope above $100,000 at the 15th percentile at 0.34, statistically indistinguishable from zero, against 1.90 below it. In the middle, happiness rises steadily on both sides of the line: at the 30th percentile, the slope above $100,000 was 1.21, nearly identical to 1.33 below it, and for the happiest 30 percent, the relationship accelerates above $100,000. Average the three curves together and you get something that looks like a plateau; look at any one group and you see something else.

Why the First Study Saw a Ceiling

Kahneman and Deaton’s measure was the problem. Gallup asked yes-or-no questions about the previous day: did you feel enjoyment, did you smile a lot, did you feel stressed? At high incomes, the average answer on the positive items was already 89 percent of a perfect score: a test that nearly everyone aces cannot tell people apart. The authors compare it to a memory test every healthy older adult passes: it can detect dementia, but not who has the better memory among healthy people.

Read that way, Gallup’s questions measured unhappiness, not happiness, since they could separate the miserable from the content, the group where the plateau shows up: in the 2010 data, 19 percent reported negative affect in the flat region and 11 percent no positive affect, close to the 15 to 20 percent the reanalysis identifies. Killingsworth’s study had the opposite blind spot: fine-grained momentary measures, but averages that hid the plateau group just as surely as Gallup’s ceiling hid the rising group.

The Doubling Rule

Here is the arithmetic the plateau story hides. Happiness rises with the logarithm of income, so each doubling buys the same gain: $30,000 to $60,000 buys the same happiness as $100,000 to $200,000. At the 30th-percentile slope of about 1.3 points per unit of log income, one doubling is worth roughly 0.9 points on the paper’s 100-point scale. The plateau story said the $100,000 to $200,000 doubling buys nothing. The reanalysis says it buys the same 0.9 points as the first doubling. Across the full span, from $15,000 to $250,000, median happiness rises by about five points. That is the whole effect. Real, steady, small.

Scale the split to the country and the stakes sharpen, since the plateau group is roughly a fifth of the population: against about 260 million American adults, about 50 million for whom extra money stops helping mood past $100,000, and 200 million for whom it does not. The most quoted number in the economics of well-being described the minority all along.

The Strongest Counterargument

The sharpest reply: none of this shows a raise makes anyone happier. The 2023 paper compares different people at one moment in time, and richer people differ from poorer people in health, job security, and neighborhoods. A 2024 letter by Johannes Rohrer and Sarah Wenz argues the conclusions rest on causal assumptions the data cannot support: income may be a proxy for what actually moves mood, not the cause of it. A second letter, by Ruben Arslan, adds that the miseries behind the plateau may yet be alleviated by money spent well. The authors replied to both letters in 2024, and a preprint reanalysis argues the plateau exists after all, higher up, near $200,000. The debate is live; the paper’s own authors warn the flattening and accelerating patterns are small modulations of an already small effect: the correlation between happiness and log income is 0.09.

The sample narrows the claim: everyone in the data was employed, American, and willing to install a happiness-tracking app, the median age was 33, the unemployed and retired are barely in the picture, and the data thin out below $15,000, exactly where money matters most.

What We Didn’t Prove

No one in this research was given a raise: the paper shows that happier moments cluster at higher incomes, not that a bigger paycheck produces them, and it covers the United States and employed adults only. It measures how people feel right now, not how they judge their lives, and life evaluation never plateaued even in 2010. The 15 to 20 percent plateau group is a slice of a distribution, not a diagnosis.

The Bottom Line

The $75,000 plateau was never a measurement, only the midpoint of a survey bracket, seen through questions that could not register degrees of happiness. The joint reanalysis says money keeps buying day-to-day happiness past $100,000 for about four in five people, stops helping the unhappiest fifth, and buys less than the folklore promises either way: five points on a hundred-point scale for a sixteen-fold raise.

What You Can Do

Below $100,000, the steepest gains per dollar sit at the lower end, especially for the least happy, and a raise, a shorter commute, or debt paid down are not consolation prizes. Do not treat $75,000 or $100,000 as a finish line: for most people there is no cliff past it, only the same slow climb of about a point per doubling, so weigh the next doubling against its cost in hours and stress. If you are in the unhappy fifth, the paper suggests spending on what money cannot fix directly: therapy, time with people you love, treatment for depression, since money still helps through what it buys, not the number in the account.
