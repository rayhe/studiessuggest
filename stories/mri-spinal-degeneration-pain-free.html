Your MRI Says 'Degenerative Disc Disease.' A 3,110-Person Review Found It in 96% of 80-Year-Olds With No Back Pain

Radiologists routinely report degenerative changes as if they explained back pain. A systematic review of 3,110 adults with no back pain found disc degeneration in 37 percent of 20-year-olds and 96 percent of 80-year-olds, which suggests the scariest line on the report is mostly a birthday.

By Daniel Reyes, Musculoskeletal Health · October 19, 2026

The Scariest Line on the Report

Open a lumbar MRI report and you will find language that reads like a rap sheet: degenerative disc disease, disc bulge at L4-L5, facet arthropathy, annular fissure. Each term lands like a diagnosis, and patients understandably treat it as one, since something in the spine appears broken and the scan has named the culprit. Clinicians reinforce the reading: advanced imaging remains a common next step for back pain, and findings like these routinely steer people toward injections, ablations, and fusions. The logic feels airtight: pain has a location, the scan shows damage at that location, therefore the damage causes the pain. The problem is the control group nobody ordered, the people carrying the same damage who feel nothing at all.

3,110 Spines, Zero Back Pain

In 2015, a team led by neuroradiologist Waleed Brinjikji at the Mayo Clinic built that control group from the published literature, systematically reviewing every study through April 2014 that reported spinal imaging findings in people with no back pain. Thirty-three articles covering 3,110 asymptomatic individuals, nearly all imaged with MRI, were pooled to model each degenerative feature's prevalence by decade of life. The numbers read like a census of aging rather than a catalog of disease: disc degeneration appeared in 37 percent of pain-free 20-year-olds and climbed to 96 percent of pain-free 80-year-olds, while disc bulge went from 30 percent to 84 percent and the signal loss radiologists flag as degeneration showed up in 17 percent of 20-year-olds and 97 percent of 80-year-olds. Disc protrusion, the word patients hear as herniation, was present in 29 percent of pain-free 20-year-olds. The conclusion: many imaging-based degenerative features are likely part of normal aging and unassociated with pain.

The Birthday Math

The paper never runs the calculation its own numbers invite, so here it is. Treat "disc degeneration on MRI" as a diagnostic test for the claim "your discs are causing your pain": at age 40, 68 percent of pain-free people test positive, and a test that lights up in two-thirds of healthy people cannot be strong evidence of disease. The same team later supplied the other half: a companion meta-analysis of 14 case-control studies (3,097 adults, 50 or younger) where disc degeneration was genuinely more common in people with back pain, odds ratio 2.24. Converting the base rate to odds (0.68/0.32 = 2.13) and multiplying by 2.24 puts the implied probability in a 40-year-old with pain near 83 percent, making the positive likelihood ratio, the number measuring how much a positive test should shift belief, 0.83/0.68: about 1.2. In evidence-based medicine, a likelihood ratio under 2 counts as a negligible shift, so the scariest line on the report barely moves the needle.

For disc bulge, the companion study's strongest pain signal (odds ratio 7.54, though the confidence interval spans 1.28 to 44.56), the same arithmetic yields a likelihood ratio near 1.8: still under 2, still negligible, on an estimate so imprecise its interval spans a factor of 35. The math applies the under-50 odds ratio at age 40, where the prevalence anchor is firmest, and treats degeneration as the binary test result reports present it as.

It Replicated

Brinjikji's review was not the first to notice. In 1994, Maureen Jensen's team scanned 98 people with no history of back pain and published the result in the New England Journal of Medicine: 52 percent had a disc bulge at one or more levels and 27 percent had a protrusion, with a conclusion quoted for 30 years, that given the high prevalence of these findings, the discovery by MRI of bulges or protrusions in people with low back pain "may frequently be coincidental." Four years earlier, Scott Boden's group had scanned 67 adults who had never experienced back pain and found substantial abnormalities in a third of them, including herniated discs in 20 percent of those under 60. The pattern has now survived three decades of replication: abnormal-looking spines are the norm, not the exception.

The Strongest Counterargument

Here is the best case against everything above, from the same authors: their companion meta-analysis found that in adults 50 or younger, several MRI features really are more common in people with back pain, including disc bulge (7.54), extrusion (4.38), Modic type 1 changes (4.01), protrusion (2.65), and degeneration itself (2.24). In younger spines, where the base rate of degeneration is lower, the signal is real, so a 30-year-old with an extruded disc and shooting leg pain is not experiencing normal aging, and no serious clinician claims otherwise. The review also covers only degenerative features and says nothing about the findings imaging is genuinely for: fractures, infections, tumors, cauda equina syndrome. Nobody in this literature argues against imaging those red flags; the argument is against scanning uncomplicated back pain to go hunting. The honest synthesis, borrowed from pain-science writer Paul Ingraham's reading of both papers, is that spinal abnormalities matter less than patients and many professionals assume, but they still do matter.

What We Didn't Prove

Nothing here proves your back pain is not coming from your discs, because this was a prevalence synthesis rather than a causation study: 33 cross-sectional snapshots with no follow-up, where asymptomatic meant no pain on the day of the scan rather than a lifetime of healthy backs. The review cannot say whose findings would later turn painful, and it was never designed to. The most sobering limitation comes from the LIRE trial: a stepped-wedge randomized trial across 98 clinics and 250,401 adults that inserted age-appropriate prevalence data directly into lumbar imaging reports. Published in JAMA Network Open in 2020, the result was a shrug: no decrease in spine-related care and no reduction in procedures or surgeries over 18 months, so information alone did not change practice, though opioid prescriptions did fall.

The Bottom Line

Three decades of evidence say the same thing: degenerative findings on a spine MRI are mostly a record of birthdays, not injuries. They run more common in people with pain, especially younger patients, but as a standalone explanation for back pain they carry a likelihood ratio near 1.2, the statistical equivalent of noise. The scan can still rule out the rare dangerous causes. It just cannot tell you why your back hurts.

What You Can Do

If your report says degenerative disc disease, ask your clinician: how common is this finding in people my age with no pain? The answer, for most adults over 40, is most of them, and hearing it changes what the words mean. Resist treating the report as a work order: the American College of Physicians advises against routine imaging for uncomplicated back pain, since early scans surface age-related findings that mislead more than they clarify, and most acute episodes improve regardless. Learn the red flags that genuinely warrant imaging: unexplained weight loss, fever, recent trauma, new numbness or weakness, saddle-area numbness, loss of bladder or bowel control, a history of cancer, or pain unmoved after six weeks; everything else can usually wait. And stay moving, because bed rest delays recovery from back pain while graded activity is among the few interventions with consistent evidence behind it.
