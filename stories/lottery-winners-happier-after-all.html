The Famous Study Saying Lottery Winners Aren’t Happier Used 22 People. A 3,362-Person Study Says Otherwise.

In 1978, twenty-two lottery winners told researchers they were no happier than their neighbors, and the finding became the cornerstone of the hedonic treadmill. A pre-registered Swedish study of 3,362 lottery winners found that a $100,000 win raises life satisfaction by 0.037 standard deviations, with no sign of fading after a decade. The catch: day-to-day happiness barely moves at all.

By Theo Baxter, Behavioral Economics · October 18, 2026

The Story Everyone Knows

In 1978, three psychologists interviewed twenty-two lottery winners and reported something that felt profound: the winners were no happier than their neighbors, and took less pleasure in ordinary delights, a good meal, a chat with a friend, as if the jackpot had recalibrated what joy requires. Their paper, “Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative?”, became one of the most cited studies in psychology and gave the hedonic treadmill its favorite anecdote, repeated in textbooks for half a century. Money buys a brief thrill, the story went, and then the mind adapts, leaving you exactly where you started.

The cornerstone had a crack that few people inspected. Twenty-two winners against twenty-two neighbors is a sample so small that almost any realistic effect would slip through it unseen. The most famous null result in happiness research was, in a precise sense, a study conducted without a magnifying glass.

The Study

Four decades later, three economists ran the grown-up version. Erik Lindqvist, Robert Östling, and David Cesarini surveyed 3,362 Swedish lottery players, winners across three national lotteries with a combined prize pool of $277 million, five to twenty-two years after their wins. The design approaches a randomized trial: within groups holding the same ticket type, prize amounts were assigned by pure luck, so comparing large winners with small winners isolates the causal effect of the money. Every step of the analysis was pre-registered before the survey data arrived.

The headline finding splits well-being in two: an after-tax prize of $100,000 lifts overall life satisfaction by 0.037 standard deviations with no sign of fading, winners surveyed more than a decade after their win scoring just as high as recent ones. But the measures with a stronger emotional flavor barely budge. Self-reported happiness rises 0.016 standard deviations and a mental-health index 0.013, neither statistically significant, even though the two correlate at 0.86 in the same sample. The follow-up questions point to the mechanism with unusual clarity: the durable gain runs almost entirely through satisfaction with personal finances, which is to say the winners are not giddier but less worried about money, and that calmer verdict on their lives, once established, holds for years without the erosion the treadmill predicts.

The Arithmetic Nobody Did

Here is the calculation that belonged in every psychology lecture since 1978. A two-group comparison with twenty-two people per side, tested the standard way, has roughly an 80 percent chance of detecting an effect of 0.84 standard deviations or larger while anything smaller is functionally invisible to it, and since the Swedish study’s measured effect is 0.037 standard deviations per $100,000, the 1978 design was blind to effects about twenty-three times the size of the real one. The famous null was close to a foregone conclusion, a verdict the study’s own arithmetic had already written.

For a human-scale translation, take the usual spread of life-satisfaction scores in national surveys, around 1.7 points on a zero-to-ten scale. Then 0.037 standard deviations is roughly six-hundredths of a point per $100,000: a half-million-dollar win buys about a third of a point, a modest sum that is also real, causal, and durable, which is more than most findings in this literature can claim. The authors’ own benchmark agrees: rescaled, their estimate matches the income gradient in ordinary surveys, suggesting the money-satisfaction correlation is partly the money doing the causing.

The Strongest Counterargument

The treadmill’s defenders can accept every Swedish number and still hold their ground, because the strongest version of their case never rested on bank balances. Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton showed in 2010 that income improves how people evaluate their lives without improving how they feel from moment to moment, and the lottery results sit precisely in that groove: the evaluative scoreboard moves, the emotional one does not, exactly as the distinction between life evaluation and experienced well-being would predict. If happiness means daily mood, Brickman’s headline survives intact, because nobody in Stockholm started grinning more.

Adaptation, for that matter, is not a myth. Andrew Oswald and Nattavudh Powdthavee tracked Britons who became disabled and found genuine though incomplete adaptation; the treadmill runs, not all the way back. Brickman’s subtlest finding, that winners savor ordinary pleasures less, was never re-tested by the Swedes, who measured satisfaction rather than savoring. The new study has blind spots of its own: nobody was surveyed in the first five years after winning, so the folk wisdom about squandered fortunes is untouched; Sweden’s safety net cushions every participant; and lottery players are self-selected risk-takers, not a random slice of humanity.

What We Didn’t Prove

The evidence comes from one country, one survey wave, and self-reported well-being. Prize assignment was random within each lottery group, which is excellent for causal inference about winners, but lottery players differ from everyone else in unmeasured ways, so strictly speaking the results describe players rather than people in general, and the design cannot see the first five years, exactly the window when adaptation runs fastest and fortunes get squandered. The 69 percent response rate leaves room for the worst-off winners to be undercounted, although the pre-registered attrition diagnostics found no sign of it. And the effect, while durable, is small: no single win converts a miserable life into a delighted one. It converts a financially anxious one into a calmer one.

The Bottom Line

The most famous null result in happiness research came from a study with no magnifying glass, and the first study with a real lens finds that money does raise life satisfaction, durably, through the unglamorous channel of financial security. The thrill fades or never arrives, but the verdict stays. “Money can’t buy happiness” turns out to be half right in a precise sense: it cannot buy the feeling, but it can buy the evaluation, and for a slogan that spent forty years as a punchline about human folly, that is a substantial promotion.

What You Can Do

If a windfall lands in your lap, buy security first. The durable benefit ran through financial satisfaction, not excitement. Clear high-interest debt, fund an emergency buffer, and lock in the boring improvements before the fun ones, because the winners who were still more satisfied a decade later were the ones whose long-run finances improved.

When weighing a raise against a better daily life, know which scoreboard you are playing. Money moves the “how is my life going” judgment far more than the “how do I feel right now” meter, so a higher-paying job with a worse commute buys evaluation rather than mood, and the choice deserves the right meter.

Distrust null results from tiny samples, because “no effect found” with twenty-two people per group is a report on the study’s eyesight, not on the world. Before you update your beliefs, ask what size effect the study could have seen: Brickman’s answer was 0.84 standard deviations, roughly twenty-three times the effect the Swedes later measured.

If you measure well-being, measure both, since life satisfaction and momentary happiness respond to different forces. A policy, a raise, or a windfall that fails to make people giddier can still leave them durably more satisfied with their lives, and the second outcome is the one that lasts.
