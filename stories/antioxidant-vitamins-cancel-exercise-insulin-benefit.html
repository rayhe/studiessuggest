Your Antioxidant Pills May Be Cancelling Your Workout. In 39 Men, Vitamins C and E Wiped Out Exercise's Insulin Benefit

Exercise makes your muscles produce free radicals, so millions of people swallow antioxidant pills to mop them up. A team in Jena gave 39 men vitamins C and E during four weeks of training and measured insulin sensitivity with the gold-standard clamp. The pills erased the benefit entirely.

By Daniel Okafor, Fitness & Exercise Science · November 13, 2026

The Pill Bottle Next to the Gym Bag

Open the gym bag of a serious exerciser and you will often find a pill bottle next to the water bottle, because the ritual is familiar: you train hard, your muscles produce free radicals, and free radicals damage cells, so you swallow antioxidants to clean up the mess and recover faster, usually vitamin C and vitamin E in doses far above anything food provides.

The logic sounds airtight, and it rests on a real fact: hard exercise does raise the production of reactive oxygen species in muscle, molecules that can damage lipids, proteins, and DNA. What the ritual gets wrong is the assumption that this damage is purely a cost: the body does not treat exercise stress as an accident to be cleaned up, but as a signal.

Four Weeks, 85 Minutes a Day, Two Pills

Michael Ristow and his colleagues at the universities of Jena and Leipzig set out to test whether the cleanup crew was interfering with the construction crew, recruiting 40 healthy young men, 19 untrained and 20 regular trainers, for the same program: 20 supervised sessions of 85 minutes each, five days a week, for four weeks, with half of each group taking 1,000 milligrams of vitamin C and 400 international units of vitamin E every day while the other half took nothing.

The doses were not subtle. One thousand milligrams of vitamin C is about 11 times the recommended daily allowance of 90 milligrams for men, and 400 IU of vitamin E is roughly 268 milligrams, about 18 times the 15-milligram daily allowance, yet these are ordinary supplement-bottle amounts, which is exactly why the result matters: 60.2 percent of roughly 258 million American adults is about 155 million supplement users, so even a small fraction taking high-dose C and E around workouts is millions of people.

Insulin sensitivity was measured with a hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp, the gold standard for this question, in which the glucose needed to hold blood sugar steady while insulin is infused reveals how well tissues respond to insulin. The team ran the clamp before and after the four weeks, took muscle biopsies to read gene activity, and measured TBARS, a marker of oxidative stress, in two parts, an open-label first part with 16 men and a double-blind placebo-controlled second part with 24 men, with 39 of 40 finishing.

The Benefit Vanished

Exercise did what exercise is supposed to do, but only in the men who skipped the pills: in the unsupplemented group, insulin sensitivity rose significantly after four weeks, in the untrained men and in the pretrained men alike, with P values below 0.001 in both groups, while in the vitamin group it did not rise at all, even so.

The molecular picture matched the clamp. In the unsupplemented men, exercise more than doubled TBARS in muscle, proof the training really did raise oxidative stress, and it switched on the genes that respond to that stress: PGC-1alpha and PGC-1beta, the master switches for building new mitochondria, plus PPAR-gamma and the body's own antioxidant enzymes. In the men taking vitamins C and E, none of that happened: the pills had mopped up the reactive oxygen species and silenced the signal the muscles needed to adapt. Adiponectin, a hormone tied to insulin sensitivity, rose only in the unsupplemented men too. Ristow called this mitohormesis: a little mitochondrial stress makes the cell stronger.

The Strongest Counterargument

The strongest case against this result starts with how the trial was built. Jeffrey Blumberg, who directs the antioxidants laboratory at Tufts University, pointed out that the study stitched together two different designs, an open-label first part and a blinded second part, without always saying which numbers came from which, and that the team tested exactly two antioxidants at exactly one dose, then spoke as if the conclusion covered antioxidants in general, a leap the data cannot support, since two vitamins are not the whole antioxidant universe and the polyphenols in food were never tested.

The follow-up record is mixed. In 2014, Gøran Paulsen and colleagues at the Norwegian School of Sport Sciences ran a cleaner double-blind test with 54 young men and women over 11 weeks of endurance training, with 1,000 milligrams of vitamin C and 235 milligrams of vitamin E a day, and the cellular effect replicated: markers of new mitochondria rose 59 percent in the placebo group and fell 13 percent in the supplement group, while PGC-1alpha rose 19 percent without the pills and fell 13 percent with them. Performance did not follow: both groups improved their VO2max by 8 percent and their shuttle-run scores by the same amount, so the vitamins blunted the wiring, not the fitness.

A 2020 meta-analysis sharpened the split: pooling nine aerobic trials, vitamins C and E did not significantly change VO2max gains or endurance performance, and pooling nine resistance trials found no effect on lean mass or strength. The molecular interference is real and replicated, but the claim that it ruins your training is unproven, since fitness and strength have refused to move in the pooled data. The honest reading is narrower: high-dose vitamins C and E reliably block the insulin-sensitivity gain from exercise and blunt the cellular signals, but whether that costs you performance is still unresolved.

What We Didn’t Prove

This was one small trial, 39 young healthy men at two German universities, run over four weeks, with no women, no older adults, and no people with diabetes or insulin resistance. It tested two isolated vitamins at high doses, not food, not a multivitamin, not polyphenols, and not lower doses, and it measured insulin sensitivity, not diabetes, heart attacks, or lifespan. Four weeks is long enough to see a metabolic signal and far too short to know whether the blocked adaptation would ever have become a worse health outcome, and the trial cannot say whether taking the pills hours away from a workout, or only on rest days, would change the result.

The Bottom Line

Four weeks of hard training made 39 men more insulin sensitive, unless they took 1,000 milligrams of vitamin C and 400 IU of vitamin E a day, in which case the benefit disappeared completely. The pills did exactly what the bottle promises, and that was the problem: by erasing the oxidative stress of exercise, they erased the signal that tells muscle to adapt. Later trials found fitness still improved, so the performance data is less damning than the metabolic data, but the insulin result has held up, and since insulin sensitivity is one of the main reasons people exercise, high-dose antioxidant pills work against the workout for metabolic health.

What You Can Do

If you take a high-dose vitamin C or E supplement and train for metabolic health, stop taking it on training days, or drop it entirely and get those vitamins from food instead. Keep vitamin C near the 90-milligram daily allowance for men or 75 milligrams for women, and vitamin E near 15 milligrams, unless a clinician has told you otherwise, and do not stop a supplement a doctor prescribed for a deficiency. If you compete and need recovery between same-day sessions, ask a sports dietitian about timing, since the interference here came from daily high doses, not a single tablet. Keep eating fruit and vegetables: the antioxidants in food are a different matter.
