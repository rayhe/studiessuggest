Eating Breakfast Later Was Linked to Higher Death Risk in 2,945 Older Adults. The Clock Is Probably a Warning Light, Not a Cause

In a UK cohort followed for an average of 22 years, each hour of later breakfast timing carried an 8% higher mortality hazard after full adjustment, while lunch timing, dinner timing, and the length of the daily eating window showed no link at all.

By Maya Chen, Health & Medicine · October 26, 2026

The Story Everyone Knows

Ask a healthy adult how to eat better and the fashionable answer has, for years, involved the clock rather than the plate: push the first meal later, compress eating into a shorter window, and let the fast do the work, because if a shorter eating window helps younger adults lose weight, a later breakfast should be at worst neutral.

The Manchester cohort says both pictures are wrong in the same direction. As these adults aged, breakfast and dinner moved later, the eating midpoint moved later, and the daily eating window shrank, and only one of those shifts tracked who died sooner: breakfast, and breakfast alone.

The Study

Hassan Dashti and colleagues at Massachusetts General Hospital, Harvard Medical School, and the University of Manchester followed 2,945 community-dwelling adults from the University of Manchester Longitudinal Study of Cognition in Normal Healthy Old Age, recruited in Newcastle and Manchester from 1983, with meal-timing questions answered up to five times through 2017.

At recruitment the group averaged 64 years old and 71.5 percent were women. Average meal times were 8:22 for breakfast, 12:38 for lunch, and 5:51 for dinner, and over a mean follow-up of 22 years the registry recorded 2,361 deaths across 63,388 person-years.

Breakfast moved later by 7.94 minutes per decade of aging in the model adjusting only for age and sex, and the paper puts the shift across the full age range at roughly 45 minutes. Lunch barely moved, and lunch timing showed no link to mortality.

For mortality, the headline number needs both of its versions. Adjusting only for age and sex, each additional hour of later breakfast timing came with a hazard ratio of 1.11 (95% CI: 1.03 to 1.18). After adding socioeconomic status, education, sleep, smoking, alcohol, marital and employment status, and subjective health, it fell to 1.08 (95% CI: 1.00 to 1.17, p = 0.05).

That attenuation is the story in miniature: a real signal survived adjustment but shrank as health entered the model, exactly what you would expect if poor health moves breakfast later. Lunch timing gave 1.05 and dinner timing 1.05 in the full model, neither significant, and the daily eating window gave 1.00, a clean null.

Later breakfast also traveled with company: fatigue, oral health problems, depression, anxiety, multimorbidity, difficulty preparing meals, and worse sleep were all associated with later breakfast, and a genetic score for evening chronotype predicted later meals too.

The Arithmetic Nobody Did

Start with the survival gap, because hazard ratios hide the human scale. A difference of 89.5 percent versus 86.7 percent is 2.8 percentage points, which means that for every 1,000 people in the early group, 895 were alive at ten years, against 867 in the late group, or 28 fewer survivors per 1,000 over a decade.

Then compound the hourly hazard the honest way, using the fully adjusted figure rather than the larger one. One hour later gives 1.08, and two hours later gives 1.08 squared, which is 1.17, or roughly a 17 percent higher mortality hazard, not a doubling and not a rounding error. The same calculation on the age-and-sex-only figure gives 23 percent, which shows how much of the raw association health and lifestyle explain. The drift itself shrank from about eight minutes per decade to 2.89 after adjustment, and the eating window showed no significant adjusted change.

The Strongest Counterargument

Now the case against, stated at full strength: this study cannot show that eating breakfast later causes anyone to die sooner, and its own authors say the arrow probably points the other way, with disease shifting meal timing rather than meal timing creating disease, so treating the clock as a lever mistakes a symptom for a cause.

The evidence for that reading is inside the paper. Depression makes mornings harder, fatigue delays the first meal, bad teeth and swallowing problems make eating unpleasant, difficulty preparing a meal pushes breakfast back, and worse sleep scrambles the morning routine, and every one of those conditions was associated with later breakfast here.

So a later breakfast may be nothing more than the visible edge of a health decline already underway.

The statistical fragility helps the skeptics too. The fully adjusted hazard ratio of 1.08 rests on a confidence interval that touches 1.00 and a p-value of 0.05, and a single cohort of mostly women in two English cities, reporting meal times on a questionnaire rather than a food diary, is a thin base for telling the world when to eat.

There is also a chronotype trap. People with a genetic tendency toward eveningness ate later in this study, which means part of the breakfast signal may be a proxy for a lifetime of late sleep rather than a choice made at the breakfast table, and no questionnaire can separate a night owl who eats at nine from a depressed patient who cannot face food until nine.

What We Didn't Prove

This evidence does not prove that moving breakfast earlier extends life, because nobody in this study was randomized to an eating schedule, and the authors present later meal timing as a marker of health, not as a tested treatment.

It does not prove the finding travels beyond its cohort: 2,945 older adults in Newcastle and Manchester, followed from 1983 to 2017, in a food environment that predates modern ultra-processed food and fasting culture, with meal times self-reported on a questionnaire that could mistake a skipped meal for a late one.

It does not prove a mechanism, since the study measured when people said they ate, not what they ate, and it did not test whether circadian misalignment, appetite loss, undernutrition, or the illnesses themselves explain the link. It says nothing about younger adults.

The Bottom Line

What survives the critics is humbler than the social media version, and more useful. In older adults, breakfast time drifts later with age, that drift travels with illness and an evening chronotype, and the people whose breakfasts drift latest die sooner at a rate that survives adjustment but barely, while eating-window length predicts nothing.

The practical meaning is diagnostic, not dietary: a sliding breakfast is cheap, visible information, and it should prompt a question about what changed, not a lecture about willpower.

What You Can Do

If an older relative's breakfast keeps getting later, treat it as a check-in prompt, not a scolding. Ask about sleep, mood, appetite, teeth, and whether cooking has become difficult, and bring a pattern, not a single late morning, to their clinician.

Keep your own breakfast time steady if you can, without forcing it. A regular morning meal anchors the day for older adults, and consistency costs nothing, but an alarm-clock breakfast eaten in misery is not what this study tested.

If you follow a fasting schedule and you are older or living with multiple conditions, do not assume a younger adult's trial applies to you. Raise it with your clinician, and watch for unintended weight loss or meals getting harder.

Track the cheap signals together. Breakfast time, body weight, sleep quality, and mood, noted once a week, will catch a slide months before any single symptom looks serious on its own.

Clinicians and caregivers: add meal timing to the informal vital signs. A patient whose breakfast moved from 8:00 to 9:30 over two years is telling you something; find out what before prescribing an earlier alarm.
