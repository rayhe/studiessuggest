Can 20 Minutes of Tetris Really Blunt Traumatic Flashbacks? After 16 Years of Trials, the Honest Answer Is Still Unsettled

Car-crash victims who played the game in the emergency department logged 62 percent fewer intrusive memories; intensive-care nurses went from a median of 10 flashbacks a week to 1. Then a 433-person preregistered replication found no lasting effect. Here is where the evidence actually stands.

By Maya Chen, Cognitive Science · October 20, 2026

The Absurd-Sounding Idea

Picture it: fresh from a car crash, a researcher in the emergency department hands you a console and asks you to play Tetris. Not for distraction or comfort, but as treatment. The claim sounds like a prank, yet it rests on a serious theory of how traumatic memories form: flashbacks are overwhelmingly visual, sudden replays of what the eyes saw. Forming those replays requires visuospatial working memory, the same mental sketchpad Tetris occupies completely. Flood that sketchpad with falling blocks in the hours after trauma, the theory goes, and the traumatic images cannot consolidate properly, leaving the factual memory intact while stripping the involuntary replays that make the aftermath so disruptive, the exact suffering the intervention aims to prevent. The memory stays; the replays wither.

The Lab Years

In 2009, Emily Holmes and colleagues at Oxford showed 40 volunteers a distressing film and had half play Tetris for ten minutes; over the next week, the Tetris group reported fewer than half as many intrusive flashbacks as the no-task group, with details remembered equally well. A 2010 follow-up: Tetris beat a verbal pub quiz, suggesting a visual effect rather than generic distraction.

The most striking lab result came in 2015, when Ella James and colleagues waited a day after the trauma film, reactivated the memory briefly, then had participants play Tetris. Intrusive memories were, in the authors' words, virtually abolished over the following week. Neither worked alone; both were required, which fit the proposed reconsolidation mechanism: recall makes a consolidated memory malleable, and the game interferes as it restabilizes.

Out of the Lab

Lab films are not car crashes, so the team took the protocol into hospitals, starting with a 2017 proof-of-principle trial in women after emergency caesarean sections that found fewer intrusive memories in the Tetris group. Then the flagship: in 2018, Lalitha Iyadurai and colleagues randomized 71 car-crash victims in an Oxford emergency department, within six hours of the crash, to a trauma reminder plus 20 minutes of Tetris or an attention-placebo activity log. The Tetris group logged 62 percent fewer intrusive memories over the following week, and their intrusions faded faster: a big number from a small trial.

The results kept scaling up: a 2023 randomized trial with 86 UK intensive-care staff traumatized during COVID found immediate access produced a median of 1 intrusive memory in week four versus 10 in the waitlist group (incidence rate ratio 0.31), alongside lower PTSD, anxiety, and insomnia scores. A 2024 trial with 144 Swedish healthcare workers tested one guided 35-minute remote session, with fewer intrusions at every point out to six months. A 2023 meta-analysis judged visuospatial interventions reduce intrusions overall, though every included study carried at least some risk of bias.

The Pattern Nobody Plotted

Line the trials up by sample size and a pattern emerges that no single paper emphasizes: the two smallest trials, the 2009 lab study (n=40) and the 2018 ER trial (n=71), reported the most dramatic effects, with flashbacks roughly halved and intrusions down 62 percent. The mid-sized trials (n=86, n=144) still found large effects on intrusion counts, but then the largest and most rigorous test arrived: a preregistered multilab replication with 433 participants. It replicated the immediate effect. Fewer intrusions right after playing, but no reduction in daily intrusions across the following week. The replication authors flagged the same gradient indirectly: among 15 lab experiments, only one was preregistered, and group sizes ran 10 to 40. Small unpreregistered studies reported big effects. The one big preregistered study returned a null on the key outcome: the classic signature of an inflated literature, visible without any new statistics, a familiar story.

The Strongest Counterargument

The best case against the Tetris finding is now the 2025 multilab replication by Wessel and colleagues, published in Collabra: Psychology: a preregistered, multi-site trial of 433 participants with an active vigilance control instead of passive sitting, in which Tetris reduced retrospective intrusion ratings during the task itself, then nothing over the week. The authors' conclusion was blunt enough to put in the title: no convincing evidence that Tetris reduces daily intrusions of a trauma film. They are not alone. A 2024 crossover trial in long-term PTSD patients found neither Tetris nor a verbal control beat no intervention. A 2022 study of 110 participants found visuospatial tasks might trim intrusion frequency but did nothing for the intensity or distress of remaining intrusions, arguably the outcome patients care about most. And a submitted commentary by Halvorsen, Wessel, and Cristea calls clinical implementation premature.

The proponents' replies deserve a fair hearing: the replication used a new trauma film and an active control, where small procedural differences can matter, while real-trauma trials keep producing positive results in clinical populations the lab work never had. But the asymmetry cuts both ways: the clinical trials are small, necessarily unblinded, and only one reported significant reductions on clinical symptom measures rather than diary counts. The honest position is that the field holds a stack of positive small trials on one side of the scale and a single large preregistered null on the other, and in evidence-based medicine the large null is the heavier object, and any honest accounting of this literature has to say so out loud. Large nulls count heavily.

What We Didn't Prove

Nothing here shows that Tetris treats PTSD: the trials targeted intrusive memories in the days and weeks after trauma, not the full disorder, and PTSD cannot even be diagnosed until a month has passed, which means none of these trials could measure the disorder they are informally discussed as preventing. The window is narrow: roughly six hours post-trauma for the consolidation version, or deliberate memory reactivation with a trained facilitator for the reconsolidation version. The studies say nothing about complex or repeated trauma, childhood trauma, or primarily verbal intrusions. And most trials used self-kept intrusion diaries, vulnerable to expectation effects when participants know their group.

The Bottom Line

Sixteen years on, the Tetris finding sits in the most uncomfortable position in science: too replicated in clinics to dismiss, too fragile in the lab to trust. The small clinical trials say a free puzzle game can cut flashbacks dramatically; the largest preregistered test says the week-long effect may not exist. Until a large, preregistered clinical trial settles it, Tetris for trauma is a fascinating hypothesis with promising pilot data. Not a treatment yet.

What You Can Do

If you work in emergency care or trauma research, the implication is procedural, not prescriptive: support the large preregistered trials the field now needs, since the existing evidence cannot carry clinical implementation. If you have just experienced something traumatic, know intrusive memories in the first weeks are common and usually fade; no game is proven to change that, and this article is not medical advice. If flashbacks persist beyond a month, interfere with sleep or work, or come with avoidance and hypervigilance, talk to a clinician about evidence-based PTSD treatments like trauma-focused CBT or EMDR, which have large trial literatures behind them. Treat the next miracle-game headline with the skepticism it has earned: ask for the sample size, whether it was preregistered, and what the control group did. Ask all three.
