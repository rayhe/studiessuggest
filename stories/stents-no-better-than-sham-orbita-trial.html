Heart Stents Didn’t Beat a Fake Operation. A 200-Patient Sham Trial Found the Relief Was Mostly Placebo

In 2017, Imperial College researchers gave 200 angina patients either a real stent or a fake procedure, with headphones on and nothing implanted. The stents fixed the arteries. The patients felt almost exactly the same.

By Elena Marsh, Cardiology & Health · November 27, 2026

📋 The Study

The Operation Everyone Assumed Worked

Chest pain walks into a cardiology clinic, an angiogram shows a narrowed artery, and a stent props it open. The patient feels better, the cardiologist watches it happen, and everyone is satisfied. That sequence plays out more than 300,000 times a year for stable angina alone, and it rests on logic so tidy it barely seems to need testing. Open the pipe. Restore the flow. Relieve the pain.

The problem was that nobody had ever tested it properly. Every earlier trial compared stenting against pills with everyone knowing who got what, and patients who know they got a new device tend to report feeling better. Drugs have to beat a sugar pill before they reach the market. Devices never faced the same test, because faking an operation sounded unethical. In 2017, a team at Imperial College London decided to do it anyway.

A Fake Operation, Done Honestly

ORBITA enrolled 230 adults with stable angina and a severe narrowing, at least 70 percent, in a single coronary artery. Before randomization, every patient spent six weeks having medicines optimized by consultant cardiologists, with one to three phone calls a week, until most were taking around three antianginal drugs. Then the trial began: 200 patients randomized, 105 to a real stent, 95 to a sham.

The sham was thorough: both groups received sedation, dual antiplatelet drugs, and over-ear headphones. In the placebo arm, the catheters went in, the team waited at least fifteen minutes, and they came back out with nothing implanted. Nobody measuring the outcomes knew who had received what. The blinding held: at discharge, every placebo patient answered “don’t know” when asked which procedure they had received. Six weeks later, everyone repeated the treadmill test, questionnaires, and stress echo.

The Artery Improved. The Patient Did Not.

After six weeks, the stented group walked 28.4 seconds longer on the treadmill. The sham group improved by 11.8 seconds. The gap was 16.6 seconds, with a 95 percent confidence interval from minus 8.9 to plus 42.0 and a p-value of 0.200. The trial could not distinguish the stent from the fake operation, and the secondary endpoints agreed: angina frequency, physical limitation, quality of life, and peak oxygen uptake showed no significant difference, and the quality-of-life score landed on exactly zero; no one died in either group.

Here is the twist: the stents worked exactly as advertised on the plumbing, since fractional flow reserve, a pressure-based measure of blockage, rose from 0.69 to 0.90, the instantaneous wave-free ratio rose from 0.76 to 0.95, and stress echocardiograms showed the heart muscle moving better, with a p-value below 0.0001. The artery was fixed. The patient did not feel it.

The placebo share is striking: of the 28.4 seconds gained after a real stent, 11.8 seconds, about 42 percent, also appeared after a fake one, and the trial was designed to detect a 30-second difference, though the observed gap fell well short. For scale, one antianginal pill adds 48 to 55 seconds in placebo-controlled trials, roughly three times the extra gain the stent delivered on top of optimized pills. One endpoint favored the stent: 49.5 percent were angina-free at six weeks, against 31.5 percent after the sham, an 18-point gap that works out to about six patients stented for one extra person to be angina-free.

The Sequel Complicates the Story

ORBITA has a sequel, and it found the opposite result. ORBITA-2, published in 2023, took 301 patients off their antianginal drugs before randomizing them. This time the stent beat the sham, and the angina symptom score was 2.9 in the stent group against 5.6 after the sham, an odds ratio of 2.21, and 40 percent of stented patients were angina-free at twelve weeks against 15 percent. Exercise time improved by 59.5 seconds. Yet even here, 59 percent still had some angina at twelve weeks, and the honest synthesis is narrower than the 2017 headlines: stents relieve angina, but on top of well-tuned pills the extra relief is small and largely placebo.

A stratified analysis found the real benefit concentrated in patients with the worst ischemia on stress echocardiography, about three times as likely to report less frequent angina. ISCHEMIA, a 5,179-patient trial from 2020, found an invasive strategy did not reduce heart attacks or death compared with medicines alone over four years: the primary composite occurred in 13.3 percent of the invasive group and 15.5 percent of the medical group.

The Strongest Counterargument

The case against ORBITA is serious, and it starts with who was in the trial: nearly a third of patients had fractional flow reserve above 0.80, the threshold below which a narrowing counts as flow-limiting, a similar share had instantaneous wave-free ratios above 0.89, and about 11 percent were already angina-free by randomization after a drug optimization phase far more intensive than routine care, with consultant phone calls one to three times a week. Critics, including Columbia interventionalist Martin Leon, argued the patients were too well to show a benefit. There is a second problem: “Not significant” is not “no effect.” The trial was powered to detect a 30-second difference, and the confidence interval stretched to plus 42 seconds, so a real benefit of up to forty seconds remains possible. Two hundred patients, six weeks, one vessel: the trial cannot exclude a modest true effect, a long-term effect, or a benefit in multivessel disease. The stress-echo analysis partly concedes the first point, since the sickest hearts benefited most.

What We Didn’t Prove

ORBITA was small, short, and narrow: it enrolled only single-vessel disease, followed patients for six weeks, and measured symptoms and exercise time, not heart attacks or death, and it says nothing about stenting during a heart attack, where the benefit is proven. The patients were treated in five UK hospitals between 2014 and 2017, with the stents and drugs of that era. It also cannot separate how much of the placebo response comes from the procedure’s ritual versus time and attention alone.

The Bottom Line

The stent is not a fraud: it opens arteries, and when the pills are stripped away it relieves angina better than a fake procedure. But the everyday experience that built its reputation, a patient with chest pain who feels better after a stent, turns out to rest heavily on the most powerful drug in medicine: the belief that something was done. On top of well-tuned medicines, the extra relief is small, and much of what patients feel is ritual, not metal. That does not mean nobody should get one. It means the decision deserves a real conversation about pills first.

What You Can Do

If you have stable angina, ask whether your medicines are fully optimized before a stent is discussed: the trial’s regimen was far more thorough than routine care. Ask about your ischemia testing: the benefit concentrates in patients with the most severe ischemia on stress testing. If you do have a stent, keep taking the medicines: the results rest on the combination, not the device alone. If you are offered a stent for stable chest pain, ask what outcome it is meant to improve: for a heart attack, the case is proven; for stable symptoms, the gain over pills is modest and partly placebo.
