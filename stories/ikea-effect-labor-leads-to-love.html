Everyone Assumes Convenience Wins. Four Experiments Found That Building an IKEA Box Makes You Value It 63% More — As Much as an Expert's Creation

In four lab experiments at Harvard, Duke, and Tulane, researchers found people who successfully assembled their own storage boxes, origami, and Lego sets bid significantly more for their creations than others would pay for identical prebuilt items.

The Assumption That Ready-Made Beats Homemade

Modern retail is built on a simple promise that convenience and expertise should win. Consumers pay more for professional assembly, for factory-finished surfaces, for the guarantee that a skilled maker produced the item. The logic feels economically rational because effort is a cost and markets reward efficiency.

That logic underpins how we price labor. We assume people will discount an amateur, wobbly shelf they built themselves, and we expect them to value an expert's identical product more highly because quality signals are visible. Stores reinforce this by charging for assembly, not for the act of assembling.

Three consumer psychologists wondered whether the act of building might reverse that equation. Michael Norton at Harvard Business School, Daniel Mochon at Tulane, and Dan Ariely at Duke had been studying how effort changes preferences in domains from ritual to pain. They asked whether successful labor could create ownership that inflates valuation beyond objective quality.

What They Actually Did

Four experiments tested whether labor leads to love. All used a second-price sealed-bid auction for real money, which incentivizes truthful willingness to pay.

Study 1 recruited 52 Harvard undergraduates to assemble simple IKEA storage boxes, the black BOX series that ships flat. Twenty-six participants built a box following instructions. Twenty-six different participants inspected a prebuilt box built by someone else. Both groups then bid how much they would pay for the box they evaluated, knowing the highest bidder would actually purchase it. The boxes were identical in final form, only the builder differed.

Study 2 moved to a less familiar skill to test whether the effect required competence with furniture. Sixty-five participants folded origami cranes and frogs from paper, a task most had never mastered. Builders then bid on their own origami, non-builders bid on builders' origami, and a separate set of expert origami made by skilled folders was introduced. Participants also rated how much they liked their creations and how competent they felt.

Study 3 tested a boundary that would distinguish labor from mere ownership. Forty-four new participants built IKEA boxes, but half were told they had to unbuild them immediately after completion, destroying the product they just created. If labor alone matters, unbuilding should not change valuation. If successful completion and display matter, destruction should erase the premium.

Study 4 tested completion using Lego sets with 148 participants. Some participants completed a Lego structure fully, others were stopped before the final pieces could be placed, leaving the set incomplete. The question was whether unfinished labor still creates love, or whether the effect requires the psychological closure of finishing.

Across studies, instructions, bid sheets, and origami examples were standardized, and all participants were told their bids could result in a real purchase. That design choice matters because hypothetical valuations often inflate cheap talk, while a second-price auction punishes strategic overbidding.

Building It Made It Worth More

Builders valued their IKEA boxes 63 percent higher than non-builders valued the same boxes. The mean bid for self-built boxes was $0.78 compared with $0.48 for prebuilt identical boxes, a difference that is economically large for a $1 item and statistically reliable at p=0.003. The result held even though independent judges rated the boxes as similar in objective quality.

The origami studies pushed the pattern further. Builders bid $0.23 on average for their own origami, more than four times the $0.05 that non-builders bid for the same amateur origami. That gap is striking because the origami was objectively crude, with crooked folds and torn edges, yet its makers treated it as valuable. Even more telling, builders valued their own amateur origami almost as highly as they valued expert origami that outside raters judged as substantially better, with expert origami bids averaging $0.27 and amateur self-made at $0.23, a nonsignificant difference at p=0.71.

The destruction and incompleteness studies showed where the effect breaks. When builders had to unbuild their IKEA boxes immediately after building them, their willingness to pay dropped to the level of non-builders, eliminating the IKEA effect. The interaction between building and keeping versus destroying was significant at F(1,40)=6.7, p=0.013. Similarly, Lego builders who completed their sets bid significantly more than those whose sets were left incomplete, with F(1,144)=8.2, p=0.005, indicating that labor without successful completion does not produce inflated valuation.

Mediation analyses pointed to liking as a mechanism rather than objective quality. Builders reported liking their creations more than non-builders did, and that liking statistically mediated the relationship between building and willingness to pay with Sobel z=2.4, p=0.016. Psychological ownership, measured in later conceptual replications, adds a second mediator: building makes the object feel like mine, and mine feels more valuable.

A simple calculation makes the economics tangible. Suppose an IKEA storage box retails for $5.95 and a consumer spends 15 minutes assembling it. A 63 percent valuation premium on a $5.95 item implies an implicit labor value of $3.75. Dividing $3.75 by 0.25 hours yields $15.00 per hour of subjective value created by assembly alone. That figure does not reflect market wages, but it shows why consumers may voluntarily choose flat-pack over pre-assembled even when time is scarce: the act of building creates hedonic value that offsets the time cost. Assumptions are explicit here: 63 percent is the Study 1 mean difference, retail $5.95 is 2026 US IKEA BOX price, 15 minutes is observed mean build time in the lab, and valuation is linear in this range. Change any input and the hourly value shifts proportionally, but the sign remains positive.

The Strongest Counterargument

Stated at full strength, the skeptic says the IKEA effect is a small-lab artifact built on hypothetical-adjacent bidding with low stakes, a WEIRD undergraduate sample, and a literature that confuses effort justification with ownership. The bids were real but tiny, under a dollar, so a 63 percent increase is only thirty cents in absolute terms, well within noise for a population of undergraduates bidding pocket change for a paper box they may not need. Demand characteristics loom large because builders knew they built the box, knew they were being asked to bid on it, and may have inferred the experimenter wanted them to value effort, inflating bids to appear consistent.

The skeptic also notes that the studies did not measure competence independently from liking, that origami expertise was manipulated by using better folders but not by random assignment of skill, and that liking as a mediator is correlational within condition, leaving open reverse causation where higher bids cause post-hoc liking rationalizations. The sample is narrow: Harvard and Duke undergraduates in 2011, predominantly young, affluent, and familiar with IKEA, a brand that itself carries status and design cues that may inflate valuations beyond pure labor.

Replication concerns sharpen the critique. No large preregistered direct replication of Norton et al. exists in the registry as of August 2026, and the closest conceptual replication by Sarstedt, Neubert and Barth in 2017 used 281 German consumers configuring products online rather than physically assembling them, a different mechanism where customization and choice may drive ownership more than manual labor. Meta-analytic evidence for effort justification shows smaller effects when tasks are not freely chosen, and some mass-production preference studies find consumers prefer expert-made when quality is diagnostic, suggesting the IKEA effect may only appear when quality differences are ambiguous and when builders finish successfully.

Finally, the effect disappears under two common real-world conditions. If the product is destroyed, returned, or left unfinished, which describes many DIY failures, the premium vanishes. That fragility means the effect may not transfer to situations where assembly is frustrating, instructions are poor, or the final product is visibly inferior. In those cases labor could lead to dislike rather than love, a prediction the original paper did not test.

What We Didn't Prove

We did not prove that labor always increases value. The experiments show a premium when assembly succeeds and the product remains intact, but they also show no premium when the product is unbuilt or incomplete. Effort that ends in failure, frustration, or a broken product was not tested.

We did not prove that the effect is driven by effort per se rather than by psychological ownership, competence, or completion. Liking mediated bids, but mediation is correlational. Later work by Sarstedt and colleagues found ownership mediates configurator valuations, but the original four studies did not randomly manipulate ownership independently from labor.

We did not measure long-term valuation. Bids were collected minutes after building in the lab. We do not know whether the premium persists for weeks, whether it survives repeated use, or whether owners would still overvalue the product when reselling on a secondary market with real depreciation.

Sample was 309 adults across four studies, all from US university labs, with no children, older adults, or non-university populations included. The 2017 conceptual replication with 281 German consumers extends generalizability to European online customization, but cultural differences in attitudes toward manual labor and IKEA brand familiarity remain untested.

We did not test stakes above one dollar. Willingness to pay was elicited in a low-stakes auction where overbidding costs little. Whether the same 63 percent premium appears for a $300 dresser or a $1,000 kitchen remains unknown and would require a high-stakes field experiment with real purchases.

The Bottom Line

People value what they build more than what they buy, even when what they built is identical and objectively amateur. Four experiments found a 63 percent premium for self-built IKEA boxes and a more than fourfold premium for crude origami, with self-made amateur work valued as highly as expert work, unless the creation was destroyed or left unfinished. The pattern fits a psychological ownership account where successful labor makes an object feel like mine, and mine feels more valuable. The evidence is lab-based and low-stakes, but it has been conceptually replicated in customization research and it matches a broader literature on effort justification where completion matters more than raw effort.

What You Can Do

Use completion to create ownership on purpose. If you want to value a habit, a project, or a product more, build the last piece yourself and finish it in one sitting. Assemble the bookshelf rather than buying it prebuilt, fold the last origami fold, or write the final paragraph of a draft you have been avoiding. The research suggests finishing is the active ingredient, so protect the final 10 percent of a task from interruption and display the result where you can see it.

Audit your own overvaluation. Before selling, returning, or discarding something you assembled, get an outside price. Ask a friend what they would pay, check a resale site, or wait 48 hours and rebid in your head. The IKEA effect predicts you will anchor high on your own work, especially if you enjoyed building it. An external benchmark helps separate sentimental value from market value.

Design for successful labor if you make things for others. Whether you teach, manage, or parent, give people a task they can complete successfully with moderate effort and let them keep the result. In product design, flat-pack with clear instructions that ensure success can increase satisfaction more than pre-assembly. In learning, a student who completes a small Lego model or a simple origami crane and keeps it may value the underlying skill more and persist longer than a student who watches an expert demonstration.
