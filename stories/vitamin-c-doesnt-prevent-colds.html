Vitamin C Won't Stop You Catching a Cold. 11,306 People in 29 Trials Say So, and a Nobel Laureate Started the Whole Thing

Two-time Nobel laureate Linus Pauling told the world in 1970 that megadoses of vitamin C would keep colds away. A Cochrane review of 29 placebo-controlled trials and 11,306 people found the vitamin does not change whether you catch a cold at all.

By Priya Raman, Nutrition & Health · November 7, 2026

📋 The Study

TitleVitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold
AuthorsHarri Hemilä and Elizabeth Chalker, 2013
InstitutionUniversity of Helsinki, Finland; Curtin University, Australia
JournalCochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Issue 1, Art. No. CD000980

MethodSystematic review and meta-analysis of placebo-controlled randomized trials testing 0.2 g/day or more of vitamin C, either as daily prevention or as treatment started after symptoms appeared

The Advice in Every Medicine Cabinet

Take vitamin C. The advice arrives with every sore throat, usually from someone holding out an orange tablet that fizzes in a glass of water. It feels like the most settled fact in home medicine: vitamin C fights colds, so taking more should mean fewer colds, shorter colds, milder colds. The logic is tidy, the remedy is cheap, and the side effects are nearly nonexistent.

It does not work. Not in the way the advice promises. A Cochrane review that pooled every placebo-controlled trial it could find, 29 trials and 11,306 people, found that taking vitamin C every day had no effect on whether you caught a cold. The vitamin shortened colds slightly once they arrived, by about 8 percent in adults, but it did not keep a single cold from happening.

The Nobel Laureate Who Started It

The idea did not come from a folk remedy. It came from Linus Pauling, a chemist who won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1954 and the Nobel Peace Prize in 1962. In 1970 he published Vitamin C and the Common Cold, arguing that gram-scale daily doses, far above the 75 to 90 milligrams in the recommended daily allowance, would prevent colds and flu, and Pauling took gram-scale doses himself, his prestige carrying the claim into millions of medicine cabinets before any large trial had tested the dose he recommended. The claim was testable, and researchers spent the next four decades testing it. By 2013, Hemilä and Chalker had 72 placebo-controlled trials to sort through. Twenty-nine addressed the central question: does taking vitamin C every day reduce the number of colds you catch?

What 29 Trials Actually Found

The pooled result was a relative risk of 0.97, meaning the vitamin C group caught colds at 97 percent of the rate of the placebo group, a 3 percent reduction that sits inside the margin of error, and the 95 percent confidence interval ran from 0.94 to 1.00, compatible with a benefit as large as 6 percent or with none at all. In plain terms: the same number of colds either way. Nothing changed.

American adults catch two to three colds a year, and with roughly 260 million adults in the country, that is something like 650 million colds a year, so a 3 percent reduction, if it were real, would prevent about 20 million of them, but the confidence interval includes zero, which means the true number could be anywhere from zero to about 39 million. Routine supplementation is not justified.

The duration finding is real but small. Across 31 comparisons and 9,745 cold episodes, regular vitamin C shortened colds by 8 percent in adults and 14 percent in children, which on a seven-day cold is about half a day less of symptoms for an adult and about a day for a child. Starting vitamin C only after symptoms appeared, the way most people actually use it, showed no consistent effect on duration or severity at all.

The One Exception That Proves the Rule

Five trials with 598 participants told a different story. These were people under short bursts of extreme physical stress: marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers on subarctic exercises. In that group, regular vitamin C cut the risk of catching a cold roughly in half, a relative risk of 0.48 with a confidence interval of 0.35 to 0.64. Extreme exertion and cold exposure temporarily depress immune function, and vitamin C is concentrated in the white blood cells that fight infection, so the mechanism is plausible, and if you are about to run an ultramarathon in the cold, the evidence is on your side.

Why the Body Caps the Benefit

There is a biochemical reason the megadose logic fails. A 1996 study at the National Institutes of Health, led by Mark Levine, fed healthy volunteers increasing doses and measured what reached their blood, finding that plasma levels rose steeply up to about 200 milligrams a day and then flattened as the kidneys excreted the excess, while white blood cells saturated at roughly 100 milligrams a day. A 1,000-milligram tablet does not deliver ten times the vitamin C of a 100-milligram dose to the cells that need it. Most of it leaves in the urine. Deficiency hurts and surplus does not: a 2022 reanalysis of the Sheffield study, a wartime experiment in which volunteers were deliberately deprived of vitamin C, found that deprivation lengthened colds by 77 percent and cut the recovery rate by 60 percent.

The Strongest Counterargument

The strongest case for vitamin C does not dispute the Cochrane numbers. It disputes the dosing. In 2005, Steve Hickey and Hilary Roberts argued that the trials tested the wrong regimen, because one to three large doses a day cannot keep blood levels high when vitamin C clears from the blood in hours, so the right test would use frequent small doses through the day, and Hemilä replied that the trials already covered a wide range of doses and schedules, that no trial of frequent dosing had ever shown the large benefit the critics predicted, yet two decades later that frequent-dosing trial still has not been run at scale.

A second problem is that the literature itself has been polluted: two later meta-analyses reporting stronger benefits were retracted, in 2021 and 2023, after readers found calculation errors. Those retractions warn about the supplement literature, not the Cochrane review.

What We Didn’t Prove

The review covers prevention and treatment of the common cold, not other infections, and it says nothing about vitamin C for pneumonia, sepsis, or COVID-19. Most trials enrolled healthy, well-nourished adults, so the results may not apply to people with poor diets. The extreme-stress finding rests on only 598 people in five trials, and the duration benefit, while consistent, is small enough that some researchers question whether it is clinically meaningful.

The Bottom Line

The advice in the medicine cabinet is half right and half wrong. Vitamin C will not keep you from catching a cold, and no dose tested in 29 trials changed that, which means the orange tablet buys you, at most, about half a day less of symptoms when a cold arrives anyway. The one exception is narrow: if you are about to subject your body to brief, extreme physical stress, the vitamin genuinely halves your risk. A Nobel Prize in one field does not make every claim in another field true.

What You Can Do

Stop buying vitamin C to prevent colds. If you already eat a normal diet with fruit and vegetables, you are getting the 75 to 90 milligrams a day your immune cells can actually use. A modest 200-milligram daily dose beats a gram-scale tablet if you want the small duration benefit.

Save the supplement for the exception the evidence supports: a daily dose around a marathon, a hard ski trip, or another short period of extreme cold and exertion is the one use with real trial support. If you do catch a cold, skip the megadose at the first sniffle: trials of vitamin C started after symptoms appeared showed no consistent benefit. Rest, fluids, and honey for the cough have better evidence behind them.
