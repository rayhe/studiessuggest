Build It Yourself and It Feels Worth More. Builders Paid 63% More for the Same IKEA Box, and Priced Their Own Origami as Highly as an Expert's

Economists assume labor is a cost: if you assemble it yourself, it should be worth less. In four experiments, Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely found the opposite. People who built a plain IKEA box paid 63 percent more for it than people handed the identical finished box, and amateur origami folders priced their own crumpled frogs nearly as highly as an expert's.

By Jonah Reyes, Psychology & Behavioral Science · November 15, 2026

The Price of Doing It Yourself

A rational shopper treats effort as a cost, so if a bookshelf arrives in a flat box with forty minutes of assembly, the bookshelf should be worth less than the same shelf delivered assembled, because the buyer is doing unpaid work. Furniture stores price that way, and Build-a-Bear charges a premium while handing the customer the labor. The logic is tidy. It is wrong in a specific and measurable way.

The first hint came from cake mix, when instant mixes arrived in American supermarkets in the 1950s and housewives resisted them, because a cake that needed no work made the cook's skill feel beside the point, so manufacturers added a fresh egg to the recipe, and sales rose. The egg was not about flavor but about letting the cook keep a hand in the work, and Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely wondered whether that small dose of labor could change what a product was worth to its maker.

Four Experiments, Three Kinds of Crumpled Paper

The first test used the most ordinary object they could find, as fifty-two students at a southeastern university were paid five dollars and randomly split: half assembled a plain black IKEA storage box from its instructions, while half were handed an identical box, already assembled, to inspect it. Everyone then bid real money for the box, using an auction in which a random price was drawn afterward: bid above it and you paid that price and took the box home, bid below it and you walked away empty-handed, so bluffing bought nothing.

The builders bid 78 cents on average against 48 cents from the non-builders, so the same box in the same room produced a 63 percent premium from the act of assembling it. Builders also liked the box more, rating it 3.81 out of 7 against 2.50 for the people who only looked at it, and since the box was rated firmly utilitarian, the premium did not come from pride in a beautiful object but from the work itself.

The second experiment made the test harder by making the object worse, as one hundred and six students folded origami frogs and cranes and bid on their own creations, while strangers bid on those amateur pieces and a third group bid on frogs and cranes folded by experts. The builders priced their own work at 23 cents, the strangers offered 5 cents for the same crumpled paper, and the experts' crisp figures fetched 27 cents from strangers, which means the amateurs valued their own lopsided frogs nearly as highly as expert work, and at nearly five times the price the market would pay.

The builders were not merely protecting their creations: when fourteen more students guessed what the average student would bid, their estimates of 19 cents for themselves and 21 cents for others matched their own bids almost exactly, so they genuinely believed the frog was worth that much.

The last two experiments tested the boundary: one hundred and eighteen students built Lego sets of ten to twelve pieces in pairs and bid against each other in a sealed auction, with builders bidding twice as much for their own set, while students who built a set and then took it apart showed no premium at all. In the final experiment, thirty-nine students assembled IKEA boxes, except that some were stopped two steps short of finishing, and the finishers bid $1.46 against 59 cents from the nearly-finishers, with the effect holding for do-it-yourselfers and novices alike.

The Strongest Counterargument

The most serious challenge is not that the finding fails to replicate. It replicates well. The challenge is what it actually is. In 2017, Lukasz Walasek and colleagues ran three experiments with 476 volunteers who built kits, Lego models, and a wooden butterfly, and found the premium only when the builders also owned the product. That result suggests the IKEA effect may be the endowment effect in work clothes: people value what is theirs, and building makes something feel like theirs, rather than labor itself creating value.

A second challenge comes from a conceptual replication by Marko Sarstedt and colleagues, who had participants build loom-band bracelets, where the premium appeared but the completion rule wobbled, because where builders could customize their work, people who stopped halfway still valued their bracelets nearly as highly as the finishers. If completion is the switch that turns labor into love, that switch is less reliable than the original paper suggests.

A third caution is scale, because every bid in the original experiments was real money, yet the largest average bid in the whole paper was $1.46, and nobody has run the origami test on a two-thousand-dollar kitchen renovation, so the premium at real-world prices is unknown. The meta-analysis helps: in 2026, Pelled and colleagues pooled 55 studies with 5,454 participants and found a moderate average effect, d = 0.57, which means the typical builder valued a creation above roughly 72 percent of non-builders' valuations, so the effect is real while its size at big prices remains a guess.

What We Didn’t Prove

This was laboratory work with students, small stakes, and products that took minutes to build, so it does not prove that building your own deck makes the deck better, or that the premium survives a buyer with no attachment. The meta-analysis pools mostly laboratory studies, so the 0.57 effect size describes controlled experiments rather than furniture showrooms. One co-author, Dan Ariely, has had unrelated research retracted over data integrity, although no concern has been raised about these studies and the finding now rests on independent laboratories and a meta-analysis. The honest summary is narrower than the slogan: in controlled tests, finishing something yourself inflates what you think it is worth.

The Bottom Line

A plain black box was worth 48 cents to the people who only looked at it and 78 cents to the people who assembled it, while a paper frog was worth 5 cents to strangers and 23 cents to the person who folded it, nearly as much as an expert's frog, and the premium vanished when the work was undone. Labor does not just cost you time, because when you finish the job it rewrites the price tag in your head: at the origami ratio, a 300-dollar bookshelf you assembled feels worth 1,380 dollars to you while a buyer would pay 300. The gap is not in the object. It is in the hands that built it.

What You Can Do

Before you sell something you built, get a second price by asking a stranger what they would pay or checking a comparable listing, and treat your own number as the inflated one, because your valuation is the least reliable in the room. Before you buy, remember the seller's attachment is priced in, so negotiate against comparable sales rather than the story of how hard the work was. If you want to love something you own, finish a small project on it, because the effect needs completion and an unfinished project earns no premium at all. And if you run a business that asks customers to build or assemble, give them a finished product to take home, since the value they attach to it depends on walking out with the thing complete.
