People Who Swear More Are More Honest, a Study of 73,789 Facebook Users Found. A Rebuttal Says the Measure Was Backwards.

Three studies linked profanity to honesty, from a 276-person lab survey to integrity rankings across 50 states. Then seven researchers showed the honesty test at the heart of the first study may measure the opposite of what its authors assumed.

By Daniel Voss, Psychology & Neuroscience · November 21, 2026

The Person You Trust Least

Most of us treat swearing as a character tell. Someone who curses in a job interview reads as careless at best, dishonest at worst. For years the psychology literature agreed: swearing correlated with the dark triad of narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy.

Then a small crack appeared. In a 2005 experiment, volunteers rated a witness statement denying a crime as more credible when it contained a swear word. The unfiltered outburst, the theory went, signals that nothing is being edited. Four researchers decided to test whether that signal was real.

Three Studies, One Pattern

Gilad Feldman, then at Maastricht University, and colleagues ran the question through three studies at three scales, starting with the smallest and most direct: they recruited 276 American adults on Mechanical Turk, asked them to list their most used and favorite curse words and rate how often they swore, and then gave them a 12-item Lie scale from the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire, a test that treats answering yes to everything as unrealistic and therefore a lie. Feldman reversed the scale and called the result honesty. Every profanity measure pointed the same way. People who wrote down more curse words scored higher on honesty, r = .20 for most-used words and r = .13 for favorites. People who said they swore more often scored higher still, r = .34.

The second study traded the lab for Facebook. The team analyzed status updates from 73,789 users of the myPersonality app, people who had consented to share their profiles for research, measuring profanity with a word-count dictionary and honesty with a linguistic formula from a 2003 study that had sorted liars from truth-tellers at up to 67 percent accuracy, against 52 percent for human judges. The correlation came out at r = .20, rising slightly to .22 after controlling for age, gender, and network size. The average user swore in 0.37 percent of words. One in nine never swore.

The third study zoomed out to the whole country. The researchers averaged the profanity rates of the 29,701 American users by state and compared them with the State Integrity Investigation 2012, an index of transparency, ethics commissions, and accountability. The correlation was r = .35. Connecticut and New Jersey swore most and ranked near the top on integrity; Utah swore least. Adjusting for neighboring states resembling each other, the link held at r = .33.

The Arithmetic Nobody Reported

Three studies, three positive correlations, three different scales, and that consistency is the paper’s best argument. It is also where a closer look at the numbers starts to bite. A correlation of .20 explains 4 percent of the variation in honesty. The self-report figure of .34 explains just under 12 percent. These are real associations, but they leave most of the picture unexplained.

With only 50 states, its 95 percent confidence interval runs from .08 to .57, which means the data are consistent with an effect anywhere from trivial to large, and a correlation across 50 dots can be pulled around by a handful of them. The Facebook numbers are sturdier, and they carry their own quiet detail: at 0.37 percent of words, the average user swore about once per 270 words, and with 3,182 words across roughly 201 updates, that works out to about 12 swear words in total, one in every 17 updates. This is not a study of people who swear constantly. It is a study of people who swear occasionally, measured against people who almost never do.

The Strongest Counterargument

A year after publication, seven researchers published a formal comment arguing that the headline result may be backwards. Reinout de Vries and colleagues, writing in the same journal, took aim at Study 1, the only one that measured honesty with a questionnaire. Their point is simple: the Lie scale is an impression-management scale, and in low-stakes settings, high scores do not mark liars. They mark people who describe themselves in unusually flattering terms, and a long line of evidence ties those scores to honesty, not dishonesty: high scorers are rated honest by people who know them, and cheat less in behavioral tests. If that reading is right, Feldman’s team reversed the wrong variable, and the people they called honest were the ones the scale actually flags as dishonest.

De Vries and his co-authors brought their own data. In two datasets with an “I never swear” item, profanity use was negatively related to HEXACO honesty-humility, the trait built to capture sincerity and fairness, and positively related to actual cheating. Their title says the conclusion plainly: honest people tend to use less, not more, profanity. The comment did not address Studies 2 and 3, but its logic reaches them anyway: if the lab study’s honesty measure is backwards, the pattern across three studies loses its anchor, and the Facebook and state-level results must stand on linguistic proxies and an ecological correlation that cannot speak for individuals.

Feldman replied in 2019 with a preregistered experiment in which laypeople read high Lie scale scores as dishonesty, which he offered as support for the original interpretation, though it shows only that ordinary readers interpret the scale the way Feldman did, not that the scale measures what they think it measures. The debate is still open. No replication has settled it.

What We Didn’t Prove

All three studies are correlational. Nobody was randomly assigned to swear, and nothing in the data can say whether profanity causes honesty, honesty causes profanity, or some third trait drives both. The dishonesty in the first two studies is mostly white lies and self-promotion, not fraud or theft. The Facebook honesty score is a formula built from lab lies, applied to status updates written for friends. The state-level link is ecological: states are not people. The three studies were not preregistered, though the data and code are public on the Open Science Framework. The Facebook sample was young, averaging 25, and was 62 percent female.

The Bottom Line

A 2017 paper reported that people who swear more are more honest, in a lab survey, in 73,789 Facebook profiles, and across 50 states, while a published rebuttal argues the lab result rests on a scale read backwards, with its own data pointing the other way. Both papers are peer-reviewed and still standing, and neither side has the replication that would settle it. The safest reading is narrower than either headline. Swearing tracks with sounding unfiltered. Sounding unfiltered is not the same as being truthful.

What You Can Do

Do not take up swearing to seem honest; it will not work. Feldman’s team warned against exactly that reading, and the rebuttal suggests the tactic could backfire. When you size up someone’s credibility, separate two questions the debate keeps conflating: does this person sound unfiltered, and does this person tell the truth? The first takes seconds. The second needs a track record. If you swear a lot yourself, the lesson is narrower: bluntness reads as authenticity to many listeners, an asset in the right room and a liability in a job interview, and neither reaction says anything reliable about your own honesty. When someone’s words and their behavior disagree, trust the behavior.
