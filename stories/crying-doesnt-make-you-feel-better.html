The Good Cry Is a Myth. 615 Crying Episodes Tracked by Smartphone Show Tears Make You Feel Worse, Not Better

A pre-registered study from Karl Landsteiner University followed 106 adults for four weeks and measured their mood the moment they cried. Overall, crying lowered positive affect and raised negative affect, and the only thing that predicted relief was why the tears were flowing in the first place.

By Daniel Ashworth, Psychology & Behavior · October 1, 2026

📋 The Study

Title
Effects of Crying on Affect: An Event-based Experience Sampling Study of Adult Emotional Crying
Authors
Stefan Stieger, Hannah Graf & Sophie Biebl, 2026
Institution
Karl Landsteiner University of Health Sciences, Krems an der Donau, Austria
Journal
Collabra: Psychology, 12(1)
DOI
10.1525/collabra.157541
Sample
n=106 adults (mean age 28.9, 70.8% women) reporting 615 crying episodes over four weeks: 315 logged in real time, 300 recovered in end-of-day reports
Method
Pre-registered event-based experience sampling via smartphone; affect measured immediately after crying and at 15, 30, and 60 minutes, plus end-of-day
Key Finding
Crying per se showed no relieving effect; overall, crying lowered positive affect (β = -.55) and raised negative affect (β = .44) relative to each person's usual mood
Effect Size
Positive affect β = -.55, negative affect β = .44 (large effects); worst trigger, overload: positive affect β = -1.02 (a full standard deviation), negative affect β = +1.37
Counterintuition
⚡⚡⚡⚡ 4/5
Replication
Not yet replicated (published February 2026); consistent with prior experience-sampling work (Bylsma et al., 2011) and the Gračanin et al. (2014) review of lab studies

Everybody Says Have a Good Cry. Nobody Checked.

The advice is older than psychology itself: have a good cry, let it all out, and emerge lighter on the other side. Aristotle gave the idea a name, catharsis, and two thousand years of culture did the rest. Surveys find 73 to 85 percent of people agree crying helps, and many psychotherapy traditions actively encourage clients to cry through distress. There was always a crack in this consensus. When researchers asked people to remember crying, the answer was relief, but when they put people in a lab, showed them a sad film, and measured mood in real time, the criers felt worse than the non-criers who watched the same film. The field could not settle it: memory is biased, and the lab is artificial. A team at Karl Landsteiner University in Austria measured crying where it actually happens: they gave 106 adults a smartphone app, followed them for four weeks, and captured the trigger, the intensity, the duration, and mood at the moment of tears and at 15, 30, and 60 minutes after, in the first pre-registered real-time study of crying in everyday life.

615 Episodes. The Tears Lose.

Nearly nine in ten participants cried at least once during the month, averaging five episodes each. Half of all episodes, 300 of 615, never got logged in the moment and surfaced only in end-of-day reports: people routinely forget their own crying. Against each person's usual mood, crying brought substantially less positive affect (β = -.55) and substantially more negative affect (β = .44), large effects by convention that grew with intensity: every notch up the four-point scale, from moist eyes to full sobbing, pushed positive feelings down and negative feelings up, and why someone cried mattered enormously. Crying from overload, 26 percent of episodes, crushed positive affect by a full standard deviation (β = -1.02) and spiked negative affect (β = +1.37). Crying from loneliness, 22 percent, was nearly as bad. Two triggers broke the pattern: crying at media, the most common at 32 percent, brought a genuine drop in negative affect that kept deepening over the hour, while crying from harmony, like being moved by someone's kindness, was neutral at first and then cut negative affect after about fifteen minutes.

The Good-Cry Audit

Combine the trigger frequencies with the mood trajectories and an uncomfortable arithmetic emerges: roughly three in five real-life crying episodes, the loneliness, overload, and helplessness kind, leave the crier feeling measurably worse, while about two in five, the movie and kindness kind, are neutral or better. The average participant cried about five times a month, which annualizes to roughly 65 episodes a year, so around 40 episodes a year are actively making things worse. Set that against what people remember: in the classic diary study, 73 to 85 percent of participants said they felt better after crying, but the real-time data show the immediate emotional signature of crying is distress, not relief. The gap suggests the "good cry" is substantially a memory artifact: what people seem to remember is not the tears but what surrounded them, the friend who showed up, the problem that got resolved, the return to normal they credit to the crying itself. A 2008 international study of 5,096 people in 35 countries found exactly that, with relief after crying tracking social support and resolution of the triggering event rather than the crying.

The Strongest Counterargument

The best case against this study's gloomy picture comes from the crying literature itself. First, the most comprehensive review of the field, by Asmir Gračanin and colleagues in 2014, concluded there is "converging evidence for the direct, self-soothing effects of crying" at the physiological level: crying produces a parasympathetic rebound after sympathetic arousal, and the rhythmic physicality of sobbing may regulate arousal the way rocking soothes an infant. The body may calm itself even while the mind still aches; mood is not physiology. Second, relief may be slower than this study's window. In a 2015 lab experiment, Gračanin's team found criers felt worse immediately after a sad film, returned to baseline after 20 minutes, and then dropped below their pre-film negative mood after 90 minutes: delayed, nonlinear catharsis that a 60-minute follow-up could miss. Third, and most seriously, the study has no no-crying control group. People do not cry at random; they cry because something bad is happening, so the mood drop may belong to the trigger rather than the tears. "Crying made me feel worse" and "I cried because I already felt terrible" are observationally identical here. Finally, the 2008 international study's finding still stands: when crying summons comfort and the underlying problem gets resolved, people genuinely feel better. The therapist's instinct to welcome tears is not baseless. It is just misattributed: the comfort does the work, and the tears are the alarm.

What We Didn't Prove

This was an observational study with no control condition, so nothing here proves crying causes the mood decline rather than accompanying it. It is a single unreplicated study, published in February 2026, of 106 mostly young Austrian and German adults, 71 percent women, recruited partly through a crowdsourcing platform. Affect and intensity were self-reported, and the half of all episodes forgotten in real time were excluded from the affect analyses. Most importantly, "no overall evidence of relief" is not "evidence of no relief": crying at films and from harmony showed genuine reductions in negative affect. This study refutes the blanket claim that crying heals. It does not refute the possibility that some crying, in some contexts, genuinely helps.

The Bottom Line

Crying is not a reliable mood repair tool. Tracked in real time across 615 episodes, tears were followed by worse mood, not better, and the relief people remember from crying appears to come from the comfort and resolution surrounding the tears rather than the tears themselves. Three in five everyday crying episodes leave the crier measurably worse. One exception: crying at a sad movie is the one category with real evidence behind it, so your emotional-support film habit is empirically defensible. By the next morning every measured effect had vanished, which means the worst thing about a bad cry is how long the hour feels, not how long it lasts.

What You Can Do

If you are crying from overwhelm or loneliness, expect to feel worse for about an hour, and plan accordingly. Do not schedule the difficult conversation, send the important email, or make the big decision in that window. Stop crying alone waiting for relief the evidence says is not coming, and seek the two ingredients that actually predicted catharsis in the 35-country study: comfort from another person and a concrete step toward resolving whatever triggered the tears. If someone you love is crying, "let it all out" is the wrong prescription. Stay, comfort, and help with the underlying problem, because social support plus resolution is the only relief recipe with cross-cultural evidence behind it. Keep the movie nights: crying at media was the most common trigger, and negative affect kept falling for a full hour afterward. And remember the clock, because every effect in this study, good and bad, had dissipated by the next day.
